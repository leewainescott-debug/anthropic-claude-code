# Value Architecture: Research Foundation

**Purpose:** this is the evidence base for a Value Architecture capability and go to market plan. It is the research layer under the deck, covering what clients' problems actually are, what the numbers say, how the market sells against them, and where the whitespace is.

**Compiled:** 19 August 2026. Sources prioritise publications from 2025 to 2026. Every stat carries a confidence flag. **[A]** means the source is primary and recent. **[B]** means the source is primary but older or classic, so use it with a date caveat. **[C]** means the source is secondary or an aggregator, so verify it before putting it in front of a client.

## 1. The argument in one page

Most organisations already invest heavily in change; what they struggle with is realising the value from what they invest in. The evidence below sets out that chain, in plain English, step by step.

1. **Money goes in.** Software spend alone is growing by around 15% in 2026, to more than $1.4 trillion, but most of that growth comes from price increases and AI, rather than from genuinely new value being created. [A: Gartner]
2. **The business case gets approved, and then nobody looks at it again.** About 25% of transformation value is lost right at the stage where targets and the business case get set, before delivery even starts. [B: McKinsey, "Losing from Day One"] Business cases are really just documents that get signed once for approval, and then nobody references them again.
3. **Delivery can succeed on paper while the value itself never shows up.** Of about 16,000 projects in Flyvbjerg's database, only 8.5% hit their cost and time targets, and only **0.5% hit cost, time, and benefits together**. [B: Flyvbjerg, *How Big Things Get Done*, 2023] Hitting the time and budget target is not the same thing as delivering value, and in most cases the budget was never really the problem.
4. **Nobody ends up responsible for making sure the benefit actually shows up.** Fewer than 40% of organisations have a formal, repeatable process for tracking whether projects delivered the benefits they projected after go live. [B: PMI, verify vintage] Only 39% of organisations mostly or always deliver the full benefits of their projects. [A: Wellingtone, State of PM 2025/26] The project team disbands at go live, and the truth is nobody was ever really named as the owner of the benefit; people in the field call this post-project amnesia.
5. **Governance mostly tracks spending and schedule, and it rarely tracks whether value was actually created.** The iron triangle of cost, time, and quality or scope is what gets reported, while contribution to business KPIs typically isn't. [A: APM] KPMG found that 83% of M&A deals failed to boost shareholder returns even while the integration scorecards showed green, which shows that dashboards were measuring activity rather than value. [B: KPMG via L.E.K.]
6. **2026 is the year the board started asking.** 66% of boards now make further AI funding conditional on proof of return, and 43% of finance leaders are being asked for an ROI number they cannot produce. [A: RGP/CFO.com] Board level AI value reporting is practised by just 4% of organisations today, but it is expected to be standard by the end of 2026. [A: Deloitte] James Brundage of EY put it this way: "boards will stop counting tokens and pilots and start counting dollars."

**What this means for the offering:** the client's problem is that they keep approving investments and then can't see, prove, or steer the value those investments create. Value Architecture is the discipline built to address exactly that; it opens every investment conversation with one question, which is what value this investment drives for the business as a whole, and how the organisation sets itself up to actually realise it. Clients already feel this problem today, through board pressure, business cases that go nowhere, and platforms they pay for but barely use. That means the offering answers a problem that already exists in the room, rather than one we have to convince the client they have.

## 2. What clients' problems actually are

These are framed the way a client would actually say them, each one with the evidence behind it.

### "We can't prove our investments paid off."
- 89% of operations leaders say their technology investments haven't fully delivered the results they expected, even though 85% of them believe they are ahead of competitors on digital. [A: PwC 2026 Digital Trends in Operations, n=767]
- 88% of executives say that achieving measurable value from new technology is a challenge for them. [B: PwC Pulse, 2023 base]
- Only 29% of executives can measure AI return on investment confidently, even though 79% of them see productivity gains from it; the sticking point is translating something that "feels productive" into an actual number on the P&L. [A: IBM IBV 2025/26]

### "Our business cases are written to win approval, and then nobody uses them to actually run the investment afterwards."
- About 25% of value loss happens right at the target setting stage, because inflated or vague benefits get baked in on day one. [B: McKinsey]
- Optimism bias is so systemic that the UK Treasury Green Book actually mandates uplifts of up to 44% on cost and 20% on schedule for standard business cases, which means the government formally assumes that business cases arrive inflated. [B: HM Treasury]
- Benefit shortfalls of 40% to 50% are common, and shortfalls of up to 75% are described as "not uncommon"; the odds that a benefit actually overruns its target, the way costs overrun theirs, are only about 20%. In other words, costs reliably run away while benefits reliably fail to show up. [B: Flyvbjerg]
- There is a well documented failure cascade for static business cases: targets stay unclear, ownership is implied rather than actually assigned, there are no early warning signals, manual tracking fades over time, and the whole thing ends in post-project amnesia. [A: practitioner synthesis, Kiplot 2025/26]

### "We finish projects, and the outcomes they were meant to produce often don't follow."
- Bain's database of 24,000 initiatives found that only about 12% of transformations achieve their original ambition, and transformations with a dedicated Chief Transformation Officer capture **24% more planned value**; dedicated value ownership is the one measured differentiator that actually moves that number. [A: Bain 2024]
- Even *successful* transformations capture only 67% of the maximum possible financial benefit, and unsuccessful ones capture just 37%. [B: McKinsey]
- Only 39% of organisations mostly or always deliver the full benefits of their projects, and benefits management is rated the hardest discipline to embed of all of them. [A: Wellingtone 2025/26]
- One note worth flagging: the famous "70% of transformations fail" statistic is a **myth**. It traces back to an unscientific 1993 estimate from Hammer and Champy that got recycled through Kotter's later work, so it should not be used. Using Bain's figures of 88% falling short against 12% succeeding instead, and knowing why the other stat is wrong, is itself a credibility play in the room.

### "We're paying for platforms we don't fully use."
- The average enterprise runs 305 SaaS apps, spends $55 million a year on SaaS, and leaves **46% of its licenses unused**, which works out to roughly $19.8 million wasted per organisation per year. [A: Zylo 2025/26 SaaS Management Index, triangulated by Productiv at around 51%]
- Cloud waste rose to **29% of spend in 2026, the first increase in five years**, driven by AI workloads. [A: Flexera 2026 State of the Cloud, n=753]
- Idle or oversized compute typically eats up **more than 30% to 40% of Snowflake and Databricks spend**; because these platforms use consumption pricing, unrealised value is literally money burning on warehouses that nobody shut down. [C: cost-optimisation vendor consensus, directionally strong, verify per client]
- Only 42% of companies fully achieve the cloud value they expected, which is only up 5 points since 2020, so migrating to the cloud is clearly not the same thing as actually capturing value from it. [A: Accenture Cloud Outcomes, Jan 2025]
- On Salesforce specifically, only 33% of AI initiatives on the platform are meeting the ROI that was expected of them, and only 26% of customers say that most of their customer data actually sits in Salesforce at all. [A: IBM IBV State of Salesforce 2025/26, n=1,222]

### "We bought AI, so where's the value?"
- 75% of leaders rank AI as one of their top three priorities, but only 25% say they are realising significant value from it. [A: BCG 2025]
- Only about 6% of companies attribute more than 5% of EBIT to AI, while 39% report any EBIT impact from it at all. [A: McKinsey State of AI, Nov 2025, n=1,993]
- The top 20% of companies capture **74% of all the economic value AI creates**, which is 7.2 times the gains their peers see; value concentrates with the disciplined few, which makes this a genuine power law rather than a matter of luck. [A: PwC AI Performance Study, Apr 2026, n=1,217]
- Gartner predicts that **more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027**, because of unclear business value, escalating cost, and inadequate risk controls. [A: Gartner, standing prediction]
- BCG's 10-20-70 rule explains why AI initiatives fail: only 10% of AI value comes from the algorithm itself, 20% comes from technology and data, and **70% comes from redesigning people and process**, yet around 80% of companies simply layer AI onto workflows they haven't changed. [A: BCG, McKinsey]
- A concept worth using from 2026 is the **"verification tax"**, which is the finding that more than 26% of expected AI productivity gains get lost to humans checking and explaining what the AI produced. [A: Sage/IDC via Diginomica 2026]

### "Our deal didn't deliver the synergies we promised the board."
- About 70% of merging companies overestimate their synergies at announcement, and only about 30% of acquirers actually hit their synergy targets. [A: Bain, 31,000-company analysis]
- Cost synergies capture 70% to 85% of the announced value within 18 months, while **revenue synergies capture only 25% to 35%**, and take about 5 years to show up against about 2 years for cost synergies. [A/B: McKinsey, Bain, BCG consensus]
- Only 43% of companies formally track synergy capture at all, despite synergies being the entire justification for the deal in the first place. [C: verify]
- The problem is sharper for private equity in 2026, captured in the phrase "**12 is the new 5**": firms now need about 12% annual EBITDA growth, against about 5% historically, to hit a 2.5 times multiple on invested capital, because multiple expansion collapsed from 59% of returns to under 15%. Holds now average 6.6 to 7 years, and 32,000 unsold companies worth $3.8 trillion are waiting on operational value creation to justify an exit. [A: Bain Global PE Report 2026, McKinsey]

## 3. Why now: the 2026 market moment

Four forces make this the right year to launch a value capability.

1. **The AI ROI reckoning.** 2026 is the "show me the money" year: 66% of boards make AI funding conditional on proof of return, 71% of IT leaders believed they had until mid-2026 to prove AI value or face budget and job fallout, and only 7% of leaders have "established" AI ROI. [A: CloudZero/Kyriba, cio.com, KPMG Pulse Q2 2026] Gartner now recommends that finance sign off before any material agentic AI investment, which shows that the ROI gate is being institutionalised, and somebody has to build it.
2. **Value reporting is about to be table stakes.** Board level AI value reporting sits at 4% today and is expected to be standard for large enterprises by the end of 2026. [A: Deloitte] Clients who can't report value are about to look exposed at board level.
3. **Waste keeps increasing.** Cloud waste is up for the first time in five years, to 29%; SaaS waste is increasing according to 35% of ITAM leaders; and AI pricing models are making cost visibility worse, with only 26% of organisations having real-time AI cost visibility, even though those that do are 5 times more likely to have established ROI. [A: Flexera, KPMG]
4. **Private equity has run out of other levers.** With multiple expansion gone and exits frozen, operational value creation is the only path left to returns, and 58% of PE firms now deploy resources in the first 100 days of ownership, double the share that did a year earlier. [A: A&M Value Creation Report 2026]

**Measurement itself is the differentiator.** KPMG's Q2 2026 finding is the cleanest version of this: organisations with full cost visibility are 5 times more likely to have established ROI, and where the CEO is personally accountable for AI outcomes, 57% realise meaningful value against just 21% where nobody is accountable. Accountability and visibility, which is exactly what a value architecture installs, are the two measured predictors of actually getting value.

## 4. How the market sells "value": the competitive landscape

### The universal product shape
Every serious player sells the same three layer structure.
1. **A diagnostic or assessment**, which baselines the client's value position and benchmarks it against peers. Examples include McKinsey's Value Assurance 360 Assessment, SAP's CVA, and Snowflake's value assessments.
2. **A value artefact**, meaning a value tree, value bridge, or value map that decomposes a financial North Star into initiatives and KPIs. Bain's "value bridge" is a good example, phrased as something like "plus $200 million in run-rate EBITDA, plus 10 points of NPS, minus 25% cycle time"; Deloitte's Enterprise Value Map works the same way.
3. **An embedded office**, which is the anchor product: an ongoing governance function that tracks forecast against actual and works to prevent what the market calls "value leakage." Examples include Bain's Results Delivery Office, PwC's Value Realization Managed Services, Capgemini's VRO, McKinsey's Transformation Office, and Accenture's Transformation Office, powered by its Momentum platform.

Every firm shares the same enemy narrative, which is "value leakage." They also share the same underlying conviction, which is that the analytical case is rarely where things actually fail; adoption, ownership, and behaviour change are where things fail instead.

### The competitor nobody talks about: the vendor's own value team
SAP has run Value Engineering since 2004, ServiceNow has Now Value plus Impact, Salesforce has Business Value Services, and Snowflake has its own Value Engineering team; all four run these functions in house. **But these teams are motivated by the pre-sale and post-sale moment, and each one is single-vendor**, meaning they exist to justify that vendor's own deal, and they hand off once the deal closes. Databricks has no publicly named equivalent, which is a gap. Any Slalom value pitch has to position itself alongside these teams, since they bring funding and appetite to the table, while owning the part they don't cover, which is realisation during delivery and after go live, across more than one platform.

### The whitespace: where Slalom wins
1. **Value plumbing as well as value architecture.** MBB value offices run on manually reported programme status. Slalom sits inside the AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake, and Databricks build itself, so it can wire value tracking directly into the platforms' own usage data, covering consumption data, adoption data, and pipeline data. That gives live, instrumented value rather than another reporting layer sitting on top, and nobody in the research found a competitor doing this well.
2. **The vendor value engineering handoff gap.** Vendor value teams build the pre-sale case and then disappear, so there is an opportunity to own the realisation phase that they abandon.
3. **Working across platforms.** Every vendor's value engineering team is single-vendor, and MBB firms are vendor-agnostic but not actually vendor-fluent. Nobody owns value realisation across AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake, and Databricks as one single architecture.
4. **The mid-market.** PwC, Capgemini, and McKinsey all price their value offices for mega-programmes, which leaves the mid-market unserved by anything lightweight, fast to stand up, and affordable.
5. **A public, fixed-price entry point.** No competitor publishes a priced, fixed-duration platform value assessment; everything is gated behind a sales conversation instead. A public offer along the lines of "10 days, fixed fee, here's what you get" is a differentiator in itself, and Slalom already does exactly this with its CRM Health Check on AppSource.
6. **Verifiable outcome pricing.** Everyone in the market wants outcome-based fees, and the blocker is proving value in a way that holds up. Slalom's access to the platforms' own usage data puts it in a structurally better position than a strategy firm to actually underwrite fee-at-risk pricing.

## 5. What good looks like: the mechanics, kept practical rather than theoretical

### Defining value, truly
A defensible working definition:

> **A value architecture is what connects a strategic ambition to the day-to-day operating numbers that prove it actually happened.** It names the outcome being bought, breaks that outcome down into the operational drivers and leading indicators that produce it, assigns a named business owner to each one, baselines it against real operating data, and keeps the whole structure alive, through re-measuring it, re-owning it, and re-governing it on a fixed cadence, from the moment the business case is signed until long after the delivery team has disbanded. It works as a standing management discipline that keeps running for as long as the investment matters, rather than a document that gets filed away once and forgotten.

How value is defined beyond ROI, to be picked per client rather than preaching all of them at once:
- One way to define value beyond ROI is the simple split between hard and soft value, or, more rigorously, the UK public sector taxonomy of three categories: **cashable** value, which is financial; **non-cashable tangible** value, which is quantifiable but hard to put a monetary figure on; and **non-cashable intangible** value, which can be identified but not quantified.
- Another way is the **value driver tree**, which is the standard MBB approach: a board level outcome breaks down into mathematical drivers, which break down further into operational leading indicators. As the saying goes, leading metrics answer the question of what the business should do next, while lagging metrics answer the question of what that action actually produced.
- When a client needs a multi-stakeholder frame, options include EY's Long-Term Value framework, covering customer, people, societal, and financial value; Accenture's 360 Value framework; and, for the public sector, the UK's National TOMs, in which every social outcome carries a financial proxy value.
- The simplest version, the one that works directly with a client, is that **value is a business KPI that somebody owns, with a baseline, a target, a date, and a named owner inside the business**, and everything short of that is really just aspiration.

### The established discipline to stand on: credibility anchors
- **PMI's Benefits Realization Management** moves through three stages in order: identify, execute, and sustain.
- The **UK Green Book Five Case Model** covers the strategic, economic, commercial, financial, and management cases, and the management case is literally defined as covering how the benefits will actually be delivered and tracked.
- The **Cranfield Benefits Dependency Network**, developed by Ward, traces a chain from investment objectives to business benefits to business changes to enabling changes to technology enablers, and the point clients tend to miss is that the benefits actually come from the business change itself, with technology simply enabling that change to happen.
- The core artefacts that practitioners actually use are the **benefits register**, **benefit profiles** (which capture the owner in the business, the baseline, the target, the date, the measurement method, and any dis-benefits), the **value driver tree**, and the **benefits realisation plan**.

### The ten mechanisms that make value real
1. Every benefit needs **one named owner inside the operating business**, and it should never be the project team, because the project team disbands and an owner who has disbanded can't keep owning anything.
2. Every benefit needs to be **baselined before the change starts**, against real operating data.
3. A **value driver tree** breaks the headline number down into leading indicators that a manager can actually act on week by week.
4. Value metrics need to live **inside the dashboards leaders already review**, rather than in a separate benefits spreadsheet, because a separate spreadsheet always decays.
5. The business case should be **re-baselined at every stage gate**, so that each gate re-validates the benefits and not only the budget.
6. **Funding should release in tranches** tied to evidenced milestones, the way venture funding works, so that capital can be redirected before all of it is spent.
7. One senior accountable owner should hold **explicit authority to kill or pause the initiative**, following the UK's Senior Responsible Owner model.
8. A **post-implementation review**, run somewhere between a few weeks and a few months after go live, should check the benefit actually realised against the original target.
9. **Smart kills should be celebrated** and the people who flag bad news protected, because otherwise sunk-cost bias keeps dead initiatives alive.
10. A fixed cadence works best: a weekly delivery forum, a **monthly value review that ends in a go, hold, kill, or accelerate decision**, and a quarterly re-litigation with executives.

### The five anti-patterns worth naming in front of clients
1. **Benefit-tracking theatre**, meaning dashboards full of vanity metrics that produce a nice looking chart but track nothing that anyone actually signed up to own. KPMG's M&A finding is the killer illustration of this: 83% of deals destroyed value even while the scorecards showed green.
2. **Post-project amnesia**, meaning the team disbands at go live and nobody is left holding the benefit.
3. **The wallpaper business case**, meaning something signed at approval that is already irrelevant by month two. The private equity version of this is the value creation plan that, as one source put it, "becomes a wallpaper deck nobody updates after Q2 of Year 1."
4. **Ownership by implication**, meaning a benefit that sits on a slide with no named owner, no baseline, and no consequence attached to it.
5. **A VRO that acts like the police instead of a coach.** Enforcement-flavoured value offices get gamed or ignored, because the office is there to help owners actually hit the number.

### Standing one up: how we'd actually get started
- A **minimum viable VRO can stand up in about 3 to 6 months**, and the first 90 days should already report early wins. Start with a maturity assessment of the client's existing governance, keep the design ruthlessly simple, track business value rather than activity, and secure visible executive sponsorship from the outset.
- **Where the VRO reports matters.** The strongest models anchor to **CFO or Finance**, the way Deloitte's Enterprise Value Delivery does with its "impact verified as booked monthly," and the way McKinsey's value assurance is embedded with finance. Finance sign-off is what separates value that is real from value that is merely claimed.
- The natural shape for this kind of consulting engagement is build, operate, and transfer: build it with the client, run it together for a period, and then hand it over. The exit is the whole point of doing it this way; a VRO the client can run on its own becomes a reference, while a VRO the client can never run without us becomes a liability.

## 6. The four offering theses, mapped to evidence

### Offering 1: Value Architecture core, reframed business cases plus value realisation setup
**Client problem:** business cases get approved and then nobody looks at them again, nobody can prove the value that was promised, and the board is now asking hard questions about it.
**What they buy (practically):**
- *As an entry point*, the client buys a **value case**, which is different from a business case, built for one flagship investment; it includes a driver tree, baselines, named owners, and a tracking design, and it runs as a small, fixed-fee engagement over 3 to 6 weeks.
- *As a follow-on*, the client can buy value tracking mechanisms, a reporting cadence, and governance, essentially installing a "living business case," or it can buy a full VRO stand-up, which runs 3 to 6 months on a build, operate, and transfer basis.
**Evidence spine:** this rests on Flyvbjerg's 0.5% figure, McKinsey's finding that 25% of value is lost at the business case stage combined with its 67% versus 37% capture figures, Bain's finding that a Chief Transformation Officer adds 24% more value, Wellingtone's 39% figure, PMI's finding that fewer than 40% of organisations track benefits, and APM's critique of the iron triangle.
**Who cares:** the CFO first, because value gets verified in the P&L, and then the transformation lead or sponsor. Deloitte's standard of getting impact "booked monthly with Finance" is the standard to match.
**Positioning line to beat:** Kyndryl's line about "the illusion of success defined by activity alone."

### Offering 2: AI value realisation
**Client problem:** AI spend is now under board scrutiny, the client can't connect that spend to outcomes, pilots aren't scaling, and the agentic AI hype is running straight into a forecast that more than 40% of agentic projects will be cancelled.
**What they buy:**
- *As an entry point*, an AI value scan: an inventory of AI spend and initiatives measured against a value driver tree, producing a kill, fund, or fix view of the whole portfolio, plus a fix for cost visibility, which is the lever KPMG found makes ROI 5 times more likely. This runs 2 to 4 weeks.
- *As a follow-on*, the ROI gate that Gartner recommends building, meaning finance sign-off before the investment is made; value tracking built per use case; and workflow-redesign engagements, addressing the 70% of AI value that BCG's research says comes from people and process.
**Evidence spine:** this rests on BCG's 75% versus 25% finding plus its 10-20-70 rule, McKinsey's 6% EBIT figure, PwC's finding that the top 20% of companies capture 74% of value, KPMG's finding on visibility being a 5 times factor plus CEO accountability, Gartner's forecast of 40% of agentic projects being cancelled, and the 26% verification tax.
**Watch-outs:** MIT's "95% fail" statistic should only be used with real caveats attached, since it comes from a small sample, was not peer reviewed, and is contradicted by its own finding on shadow AI use; Gartner's 30% abandonment figure is a prediction and should never be presented as a fact that has already happened.
**Whitespace:** no firm has branded an "AI Value Office" or "AI value assurance" product by name yet, so there is an opportunity to own that naming.
**Slalom asset:** the existing **AI Value Platform**, which forecasts TCO and ROI, is exactly the tool layer the market is now asking for.

### Offering 3: platform value unlock, with AWS, Snowflake, Databricks, and Salesforce
**Client problem:** the client is paying for platforms it only uses a fraction of, its consumption bills are rising, and it keeps being told the fix is an upgrade it can't afford.
**What they buy:**
- *As an entry point*, a **Platform Value Assessment**: it compares the modules and capabilities the client uses against what it owns, looks at consumption efficiency and adoption, and quantifies the trapped value, producing a value case and a roadmap to unlock value without major new spend. It runs a fixed duration of 10 to 15 days, extending the proven CRM Health Check pattern, and it is **often free or close to free through partner funding**.
- *As a follow-on*, the client buys the unlock roadmap itself, funded through MAP, LiftOff, or consumption incentives depending on the vendor, along with FinOps-style value governance and ongoing value tracking.
**Evidence spine:** this rests on Zylo's $19.8 million and 46% figures, Flexera's 29% cloud waste figure, IBM's 33% Salesforce AI ROI figure, Accenture's 42% cloud value figure, the 30% to 40% idle compute figure, and Gartner and RAND's finding that around 80% of analytics and AI initiatives fail to deliver outcomes.
**The easy button, meaning partner funding, though terms should always be verified as current:**
- For AWS, MAP assessment funding runs at about 5% of projected ARR, with 15% to 25% ARR credits plus partner cash available at scale; proof-of-concept funding runs up to $25,000; and Well-Architected reviews are free, with $5,000 in credits per workload after remediation.
- For Snowflake, the Services Funds Program runs at 3% to 5%, and LiftOff funds foundation work.
- For Databricks, the Partner Well-Architected Framework introduced in February 2026 explicitly ties **assessments** to partner tiering and go-to-market incentives, alongside joint go-to-market funding.
- For Salesforce, the FY27 program introduces **consumption funds** that reward adoption, which is the natural funding line for helping a client unlock what they already bought.
The sequencing rule to remember is that funded vehicles often can't be stacked together, but they can be chained, for example running a proof of concept first and then MAP.
**Strategic frame:** vendors actually want this work to happen, because utilisation drives their renewals and their consumption revenue. Slalom's assessment lines up with the vendor's own growth motion while still sitting on the client's side of the value question. Health checks on their own are now commoditised; the differentiation comes from **quantifying the trapped value and pairing it with funded delivery**.

### Offering 4: deal and speed-to-value, through a PMI or PE lens
**Client problem:** synergies get overpromised, undertracked, and captured too slowly; private equity needs 12% EBITDA growth with nowhere left to hide it from; and IT integration is the item that paces synergy capture.
**What they buy:**
- *As an entry point*, a synergy or value tracking stand-up for a live deal, meaning an integration management office value cell that runs a weekly forum, a monthly value review ending in a go, hold, kill, or accelerate decision, and a quarterly re-litigation; or, alternatively, a 100-day value plan for a newly acquired portfolio company.
- *As a follow-on*, the technology integration work that actually paces the synergies, which is Slalom's core delivery, instrumented with live synergy dashboards; or a standing portfolio value office for a private equity client, which is recurring work rather than deal-dependent work, given that holds now average more than 6.6 years.
**Evidence spine:** this rests on Bain's finding that 70% of deals overestimate synergies while only 30% hit their targets, the gap between cost and revenue synergy capture at 70% to 85% against 25% to 35%, KPMG's finding on scorecards showing green while value burns, the "12 is the new 5" figure, A&M's finding that 58% of firms deploy in the first 100 days, and the AI expectation gap inside portfolio companies, where 70% of GPs expect an EBITDA impact but only 6% can actually prove one.
**Slalom wedge:** synergies depend heavily on IT, with an estimated 50% to 60% of synergy initiatives being IT-dependent [C: verify]. MBB firms fill this gap with productised IP, such as McKinsey's myIMO, rather than hands-on engineering, which leaves the actual build job, meaning the IMO's data pipes and live dashboards, open for Slalom.

### One methodology under all four
The same spine runs through every offering. **First, frame the value, through a driver tree, baselines, and named owners. Then fund it right, staged and evidence-gated, using partner funding where it's available. Then track it live, inside the platforms and inside Finance's own numbers. Then govern it to realise the value, through a fixed cadence, kill authority, and re-baselining.** The four offerings are simply different doors into the same room, and that is what makes this a capability rather than four separate products.

### Stats to avoid, or to heavily caveat if used
- **MIT NANDA's "95% of GenAI pilots fail"** comes from a small, non-random sample, was not peer reviewed, and is contradicted by its own finding on shadow AI use. If it gets used at all, it should work only as a hook, paired immediately with "and here's what the 5% do differently."
- **Gartner's "30% of GenAI projects abandoned by end-2025"** was a 2024 prediction that was never actually verified as an outcome, so it should be labelled clearly as a prediction, or dropped entirely.
- **"70% of transformations fail"** has no empirical basis; it traces back to a recycled 1993 guess. Use Bain's figures instead.
- **The agentic ROI multiples that vendor aggregators publish**, such as figures of 171% to 192% or 540% ROI, are untraceable marketing numbers.
- **Vendor TEI studies**, such as Databricks claiming 417% ROI or SAP claiming 251%, are only usable as a description of "the value the vendor claims is available," never as independent evidence.

### Needs verification before client use, since these are secondhand in this pass
Several figures need a proper verification pass before they go in front of a client. These include PMI's finding that fewer than 40% of organisations track benefits after go live, where the vintage and original publication still need confirming; the finding, attributed to McKinsey, that 30% to 50% of M&A value is lost to slow integration; the cluster of statistics claiming that 79%, 70%, and 63% of deals have no plan in place at signing; the finding that 50% to 60% of synergy initiatives are IT-dependent; the finding that 43% of companies formally track synergy capture; BCG Platinion's 70% figure; the finding that around 80% of features go unused; and Salesforce's "37% fully embraced" figure. Direct fetches to mckinsey.com, bain.com, bcg.com, flexera.com, and most other consulting domains were blocked by the proxy during this research session, so every finding from those domains came through search snippets instead. A verification pass is recommended for anything that will go verbatim onto a client-facing slide.

## 8. Open questions for the storyline: decisions to make before building slides

1. **Name and claim.** Value architecture is the name; the open question is only how Slalom's version gets described in one line so it reads as ours.
2. **Anchor buyer.** The evidence says CFO or Finance is where value actually gets verified, but Slalom's relationships are often on the CIO or CDO side instead. Which door should the deck lead with, and does the client-partner enablement story need to bridge the two?
3. **Lead offering.** The platform value assessment is the easiest offering to sell, since it's funded, fixed in scope, and finds concrete waste, but it's also the least differentiated category; the VRO is the most differentiated offering but the biggest ask of a client. Should we lead with the assessment as the wedge and the VRO as the eventual destination?
4. **Tooling.** The market now expects a demoable value-tracking layer, the way Accenture has Momentum and McKinsey has its own instrumentation. Is the right move to extend Slalom's AI Value Platform, to build a lightweight value dashboard accelerator native to client platforms such as Snowflake or Databricks, or to stay tool-agnostic for now?
5. **Commercial posture.** Fee-at-risk or outcome pricing is the direction the market is heading, and Slalom's access to the platforms' own usage data makes that credible, but it will need internal appetite to actually offer it. Should the deck signal this now, or hold it back?
6. **Internal enablement scope.** The stated goal is for client partners to bring Lee into value conversations themselves. Does the deck need a companion one-pager for each offering, the kind of "when you hear this, call me" cheat sheet, as part of the go-to-market plan?
