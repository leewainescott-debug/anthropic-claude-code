# Part 1. The problem

## What is actually broken

Organisations do not have an investment problem, they have a realisation problem. The money gets approved, the platform gets bought, the programme gets delivered, and somewhere between the signature and the switch on, the thing that was promised, the value, quietly stops being anyone's job. The business case wins the money and is never opened again; delivery reports green all the way through; the team disbands the week the system goes live; and nobody is left who could say what the business actually got for it.

## The evidence underneath, step by step

The loss starts before delivery does. McKinsey's transformation research found that roughly a quarter of the value a transformation could capture is lost at the target setting stage, before delivery has even started; the finding is from 2021 and still the framework they sell against, and what sits behind it is that business cases are written to win approval rather than to run an investment, so the benefits are vague or inflated on day one and there is nothing underneath them to manage to.

Then delivery succeeds while value does not. Bent Flyvbjerg's database of around sixteen thousand projects, published in 2023, found that only 8.5 per cent of projects hit their cost and time targets, and only 0.5 per cent, one project in two hundred, hit cost, time and benefits together. That last number says our industry's favourite promise measures the wrong thing. We say we'll deliver on time and on budget in project management, but the budget is never really the issue if you're not realising the benefits you set out to get.

After the system is live it goes quieter still, because nobody is left holding the benefit. Wellingtone's state of project management study from this year's cycle finds that only 39 per cent of organisations mostly or always deliver their full project benefits, and rates benefits management the hardest discipline to embed. The cause is structural; the project team disbands at go live, the sponsor moves on to the next approval, and the benefit was never assigned to a named person who would still be in the room when it was due to arrive.

The governance that should catch this is pointed at the wrong dials, because steering packs report spend, milestones and scope, and say almost nothing about the business measures the investment was bought to move. The cleanest illustration is from deals; KPMG found that 83 per cent of acquisitions failed to lift shareholder returns while the integration scorecards were showing green. It is an older finding and we would say so in front of a client, but the pattern has not aged, which is that dashboards measure activity and activity is not value.

And clients already know. In PwC's 2026 operations study, 89 per cent of leaders said their technology investments have not fully delivered the results they expected, while 85 per cent of the same population believe they are ahead of their competitors digitally. Both are true at once; everybody believes they are winning and almost nobody can show the prize arrived.

None of this means transformation is a coin toss. Bain's current database of twenty four thousand initiatives finds that only around 12 per cent of transformations achieve the ambition they started with, and that where a dedicated senior owner holds the value, in Bain's data a chief transformation officer, the organisation captures 24 per cent more of the planned value than where that owner is missing. The second number is the diagnosis hiding inside the first; the difference is not better technology or better luck, it is that somebody owned the number, and in most organisations value is the only line in the whole investment that never gets an owner.

## Why this is the year

What makes 2026 different is that the question has moved into the boardroom, and AI spend is the sharpest edge of it. This year's finance surveys find that 66 per cent of boards now condition further AI funding on proof of return, and that 43 per cent of finance leaders are being asked for a return number they cannot produce; the board number is the firmer of the two and we would verify the second before putting it on a slide, but the direction is not in doubt. Deloitte finds board level AI value reporting practised by only around 4 per cent of organisations today and expects it to be standard for large enterprises by the end of 2026, so many clients are about to discover in front of their own board that they cannot produce the number. The board is practising on AI a question it will shortly ask of every line of spend, which is what did we actually get for this, and almost nobody is set up to answer it.

## Our problem inside the client's problem

There is a second level to this and it is ours rather than the client's. Slalom is already inside these investments; we build on AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake and Databricks, we see the consumption, the adoption and the delivery, and yet the conversation about what all of it is worth happens without us, when it happens at all. When a client does buy that conversation, they buy it from strategy firms who never touch the build and run their value offices on manually reported status, or they get it free from a vendor's value team whose interest peaks at the signature and fades after the deal closes. So the client's realisation problem and our positioning problem are the same missing conversation; we need to position ourselves to have broader client conversations with more than just technology. How do we position ourselves as truly value led. Value is such a broad topic, but we need to make it easier for clients to capture value, to have the conversation on value, to realise value. The practical start of that is setting up the client partners and internal teams well so they're able to bring me into a conversation to discuss with our clients on business value, and that only works if the problem is stated so plainly that anyone can carry it into an account meeting.

## The problem statement, in the words we would use with a client

Here it is, then, in words we can say across the table. You keep approving investments whose value you cannot see, cannot prove and cannot steer; the money moves, the case that justified it goes in a drawer, delivery reports green, the team that built it moves on, and when your board asks what the business actually got, the honest answer is a list of things that went live. Most clients will recognise this before we finish saying it, and the ones who do not are often the most exposed, which is why the telling is sometimes ours to do. We need to be solving a problem as we can't just create a solution that doesn't solve a business problem. And often we'd need to tell the client with the potential problem. Nothing in this part is for sale on its own; everything a client actually buys comes later in this document, one deliberately small purchase at a time, and each one begins from this recognition. The problem is real, it is measured, clients already feel it, and this is the year their boards started asking, so every conversation this practice opens begins from one question: what value does this investment drive for our business as a whole and how do we set ourselves up to realise it.


# Part 2. What we mean by value

Before we sell value we have to be able to say what it is, out loud, in a sentence a client can repeat back to us, and it is worth being honest that our industry has not managed this. Every large firm sells value creation and no two of them define it the same way; McKinsey treats value as cash above the cost of capital, BCG breaks it into the components of shareholder return, EY and Accenture spread it across customers, people and society, and PwC runs two value practices that have never been reconciled with each other. KPMG's own report admits that value creation is "a concept often discussed, yet rarely defined with precision", and a recent academic review of 288 articles found no consensus in the literature either. The definition we use is therefore a decision rather than a discovery, and that suits us, because saying plainly what value means and holding to it is something most of the market has not done.

So here is the definition, in two halves that only work together. Value is the extent to which an investment moves the business towards something it already said mattered; a strategic priority, not a technology outcome. A system can go live perfectly, on time and on budget, and still create no value if nobody can point to the business priority it was supposed to move. And value only counts when someone in the business owns it; someone on the client side puts their name against the number, agrees when we will check it, and agrees what happens if it does not show up, because otherwise value is a number in a slide that nobody is responsible for.

The obvious alternative is the industry default, return on investment, and it fails in two directions at once; it fails on direction, because a return can be earned on something that never mattered while the business stands still, and it fails on accountability, because a ratio has no owner, no agreed date, and nobody answerable when it fails to appear. For transformation programmes, how are we actually realising the value that we set out to achieve, goes beyond roi; but what business kpis are we driving. Value is also not always money, and this needs saying plainly: value is defined in the client's own measures, and for plenty of organisations the measure that matters most is not a financial line at all but safety, or customer trust, or how quickly a citizen gets an answer. The UK Treasury's Green Book is explicit that value for money is a balanced judgement rather than just the monetised impacts, and that a lower benefit to cost ratio can still be the better value.

The reason to trust our definition is not taste. Underneath the disagreement, every definition that works in practice shares six mechanics: value is measurable, baselined, owned by a named person in the business rather than by the project team, time bound with a date when someone checks, tied to strategy, and about outcomes rather than outputs. Our two halves are those six mechanics spoken as sentences, and the pairing is what beats any single alternative, because the strategy half without the ownership half is a well framed ambition on a slide, and the ownership half without the strategy half is diligent tracking of things that may not matter.

This is also why we talk about value cases rather than business cases. Often for technology investments clients are wanting a business case to justify the investment. We need to reframe clients thinking to ensure that the investment drives business value, more than just their own dept or replacing a burning legacy platform, therefore, we need to start thinking about value cases and baselining the investment so that business value is realised beyond implementation. What strategic priorities is this investment driving as opposed to what technology outcome is it driving; allows better long term strategic decision making that will impact the business long term. The distinction is not our private language; independent practitioner writing draws the same line, where a business case justifies the spend and a value case carries the broader picture over the long run.

The definition also has to hold across every kind of value a client might name, and it does. Across everything the firms and the disciplines publish, the same seven kinds keep recurring: financial, customer, people, operational, risk, strategic and public value, and financial is the only one every approach names, while only benefits realisation and the public sector treat the rest as structurally equal rather than an add on. Our definition holds across all seven because it refuses to pick one; it asks which of them this business already said mattered, and lets the client's strategy do the choosing. When the value is not money we split benefits into cashable, meaning they release money you can bank; non cashable but tangible, meaning you can count them even though turning them into money would be artificial; and non cashable and intangible, meaning you can identify and evidence them without pretending to count them. Every one of those still passes the same test, a measure, a baseline, a named owner and a date.

There are two questions the definition leaves open, because they have to be decided per client, in the first conversation rather than by default. The first is whether value rolls up into one blended number or stays as a scorecard in native units; one school monetises everything it can touch into a single figure, the other keeps each measure in its own units and asks the board for a balanced judgement across them. Our default is the scorecard in native units, because a money proxy bolted onto safety or trust usually makes the number less credible rather than more, but where a client's governance already runs on a single number we blend, because value reported in a format the board will not read is value that does not get reported. The second is whether we are measuring the value of the technology or the value to the business, and the vendor's frame naturally measures the technology; adoption, utilisation, consumption and features switched on. The Cranfield benefits researchers gave us the clean way to place those numbers, separating the means, the ways and the ends; the technology is the means, the change in how people work is the ways, the benefit to the business is the ends, and value lives only at the ends. We track the means and the ways as the leading indicators that tell us whether the ends are coming, and we never let them stand in for value, however good the adoption chart looks.

There is one more reason this definition is right for us, and it is a gift. Slalom has already said publicly that delivery is not your win, value is, and has described value realisation as a leadership habit of translating strategy into measurable outcomes you can see in production. We do not need to argue the definition into our own firm; the firm has signed it in public, and this practice adds the machinery that makes it true on real investments.

What this means in the room is simple, and everything above is sayable without a slide. The definition resolves into four questions we can ask about any investment: which of your stated priorities does this move, who in your business owns that number, what is it today, and when do we check. If the client can answer, we have the start of a value case; if they cannot, that is the most useful conversation we can open with them, and later in this document it has a home of its own.


# The value case: the work before the money moves

This work starts from the most ordinary sentence in corporate technology, which is that we need a business case to get the investment approved. The person saying it is a sponsoring executive with a date, and the committee they are walking into has usually grown sceptical. Often for technology investments clients are wanting a business case to justify the investment. We need to reframe clients thinking to ensure that the investment drives business value, more than just their own dept or replacing a burning legacy platform, therefore, we need to start thinking about value cases and baselining the investment so that business value is realised beyond implementation. What strategic priorities is this investment driving as opposed to what technology outcome is it driving; allows better long term strategic decision making that will impact the business long term.

A reframe on its own would only be new words on the same dying document; it's more than just reframing it to a value case, it's how do we ensure that this is driving business value, how do we set it up so that we can show the value delivered and how do we ensure the value is delivered, reported. Together those two thoughts change what the case is about, a strategic priority rather than a technology outcome, and what the case is, machinery built to run an investment rather than an argument written to win a decision.

## What we actually do

We do four things on every value case. We build a driver tree downwards from strategy rather than upwards from the technology, starting from a priority the business has already stated, the board level outcome the investment is supposed to move, and decomposing it into the operational measures a manager can act on weekly, because leading indicators tell us what to do and lagging ones tell us what that produced. We baseline before a dollar moves, measuring every benefit against real operating data, because a benefit without a baseline can never be proved, and once delivery begins the organisation starts changing and the starting line is gone. We put a named owner in the business against every benefit, never in the project, because the project team disbands and the owner cannot, which is the most consistent finding in the whole benefits discipline; the owner puts their name to the number and agrees when we will check it. And we agree with finance, before approval, exactly how movement will be verified, which numbers count, out of which systems, confirmed by whom; the strongest models in the market verify impact as booked with finance month by month, because confirmation by finance is what separates claimed value from real value.

## Why this happens before approval

Because that is when the damage happens. McKinsey's transformation research finds roughly a quarter of the value a transformation could capture is lost at the target setting stage, inside the business case, before delivery begins. The optimism is so systematic that the UK Treasury's Green Book formally requires standard business cases to be uplifted by as much as 44 per cent on cost and 20 per cent on schedule to correct for it, which is a government formally assuming the cases put in front of it are inflated. And Flyvbjerg's project research shows the far side of the signature, where benefit shortfalls of 40 to 50 per cent are common and up to 75 per cent not uncommon, so costs reliably run away and benefits reliably fail to show up. A case written only to win money is inflated on the day it is signed and decaying from the day it is approved; the decay runs from unclear targets through implied ownership to tracking that fades and finally nobody left who remembers what was promised, and the four things we do are aimed one for one at that cascade, which is why they have to be in the case before the committee says yes.

## What the client buys

The client buys a value case for one flagship investment, fixed fee, three to six weeks, staffed as a pod of two or three with a senior person fronting it. It runs inside the approval timetable, because the one thing this buyer will never purchase is anything that slows the approval down; the value case is a stronger version of the document they already had to write, and a sceptical committee finds baselines and named owners more persuasive than another optimistic spreadsheet. Part of the price is paid in the client's own hours, because the future benefit owners sit in the room while their numbers are set; an owner who was handed a number never owns it the way an owner who set it does.

The artefact at the end is a small family the whole practice reuses: the driver tree; a benefits register listing every benefit with its definition, measure, owner and timeline; a benefit profile for each one carrying the owner in the business, the baseline, the target and its date, the measurement method agreed with finance, and the disbenefits stated honestly; and a realisation plan that says who does what after the delivery team has gone. None of it is decoration on the funding paper; it is the operating kit the investment will be run with.

## How the value case stays alive

The deeper failure this shape exists to fix is that business cases are just set up to get the dollars and then go nowhere and aren't living, breathing documents, how do we actually turn those business cases into living and breathing baseline and metric tracking sources that are used throughout the engagement. So the value case comes with the install that makes it live. The value metrics go inside the dashboards leaders already review rather than into a separate benefits spreadsheet, because separate tracking fades and dashboards get read; the case is baselined again at every gate, so that gates revalidate the benefits and the strategic fit rather than only the budget, which is what stops sunk cost drift; where the client's governance allows it, funding is released in tranches against evidence, so money can be redirected before all of it is spent; and a monthly value review ends in a real decision to continue, pause, stop or accelerate, not in a status update. This is also where our industry's favourite promise gets retired. We say we'll deliver on time and on budget in project management, but the budget is never really the issue if you're not realising the benefits you set out to get. A living value case makes the better promise, which is that at every gate we will know whether the value is still coming, while there is still money left to redirect.

## What it grows into, and what it costs in effort

The install grows into a standing monthly value review that outlives the programme, and by design every value case is assembled from the same components a Value Realisation Office runs on, a baseline, a driver tree, an owner map and a cadence, so whether or not the client ever buys the office, every value case leaves them one room closer to being able to run one. In effort terms the build is the pod for those few weeks and the install is a rhythm measured in days a month rather than a standing team, and because the artefact family is the same every time, the second case costs less than the first, the work can be fronted by people who are not me, and every engagement banks a quantified before and after that the practice sells on next year.


# Part 4, the middle moments: the running programme, the programme in trouble, and the platform already paid for

While the work is running there is one conversation, whether the programme is healthy or failing; what business value can this drive, how are we going to realise it post investment and implementation. The asker differs, because the sponsor of a programme with soft benefits and the executive above a programme going south would never buy the same thing, so one conversation gets two entrances.

## Moment two, the programme running with nothing to show

The sentence we hear is that the steering committee asks what value is being created and all anyone can show is milestones and spend. It comes from the programme sponsor or transformation lead whose benefits were soft or never baselined, and the date that matters is the next steering committee. What they cannot answer that week is what the business has received and what is still coming; more than half of transformation value loss happens during and after implementation, and benefit tracking fades unless the metrics live in dashboards leaders already review.

We rebaseline what is left, because the useful question is the value still available, not a verdict on the case that won the money; we put a named owner from the business against each benefit; we wire the value metrics into the numbers leaders already read, not a separate benefits spreadsheet that decays; and we stand up a monthly value review that can stop, redirect or double down where value is arriving early. It matters because for transformation programmes, how are we actually realising the value that we set out to achieve, goes beyond roi; but what business kpis are we driving, and because it's more than just reframing it to a value case, it's how do we ensure that this is driving business value, how do we set it up so that we can show the value delivered and how do we ensure the value is delivered, reported.

The client buys a retrofit of a few weeks, fixed fee, funded from the programme's own budget without a new approval cycle, leading into the standing monthly review; they would never buy anything that reads as an audit of their own business case, so we never frame it as one.

## Moment three, the programme nobody trusts

The sentence is blunter; they no longer trust what the programme is telling them. The buyer sits above it, often the chief financial officer, with the next funding decision in front of them, and the wrong promise is the reason; we say we'll deliver on time and on budget in project management, but the budget is never really the issue if you're not realising the benefits you set out to get. They cannot answer whether to release the next tranche and the programme's reporting will not tell them; benefit shortfalls of 40 to 50 per cent are common, and gates that should revalidate benefits drift into sunk cost momentum.

What they buy is a rapid independent value diagnostic, two to four weeks, fixed fee, ending in a rebaselined case and a recommendation to continue, pause, stop or accelerate, delivered to the funder rather than the programme; it leads into the same machinery as moment two. They would never buy more governance from the delivery lens that already failed them, and one rule is absolute; we only take this work where Slalom is not the delivery partner under question, because independence is the entire product.

## Moment four, the platform already paid for

Once everything is live the investment falls off every agenda, which is where the quietest money sits. The sentence is that they are paying a great deal for a platform and do not believe they are getting what they bought; there are two buyers, the platform owner facing a renewal or a rising consumption bill and the business executive whose direction the platform is meant to serve, and that bill is the dated event. We ask, are you getting the most out of any platform that you're using today, and neither buyer can answer it that week; across the market roughly half of licensed seats sit unused, and more than 90 per cent of finance teams run weekly planning in spreadsheets despite owning the platforms bought to replace them. The pattern is an organisation owning a perfectly capable platform while the teams run the work in spreadsheets beside it and go shopping for a new tool that does what they already own, and that is not a client failing but the organisational default. The conversation is an offer, not an accusation; you have already invested in this platform, but it's either under utilised or there are locked benefits or you could be getting so much more out of your investment, and often clients invest in these platforms and haven't even fully unlocked the capabilities they have available to them.

The assessment runs two to three weeks at a published fixed fee. Week one reconciles the three views, what is contracted, what is activated and what is actually used, with the platform's own usage tooling as the baseline, and runs split sessions with the platform admins and with the business users, who know where the spreadsheets and workarounds live. Week two maps what they own against where the business is going over the next twelve to twenty four months, in a session with their executives rather than against a generic checklist, because you're using this platform, your business is going in x direction, there are things we can help you unlock in this platform that will actually drive better outcomes for your business as a whole. Week three lands a roadmap split into what can be switched on now with no new build and what needs a scoped enablement phase, each item tied to a named business outcome, with a light measurement cadence so it becomes an operating discipline rather than a report; we do the assessment and use it to prove the value that is locked, and then we clearly outline the roadmap to unlock trapped value in their organisations. It leads into that enablement phase and into value tracking wired to the platform's own telemetry.

This is strategy led, not a licence audit; an audit starts from spend, reads as blame and ends in a procurement conversation, while ours starts from the outcome logic of the client's strategy, names value leakage rather than wasted licences, prioritises by business direction rather than licence dollars, and ends in a cadence, not a document. It is using strategy as an enabler for our clients to unlock value in their existing platforms without having to pay millions to upgrade their platforms, which is also what this buyer would never buy; a free commodity health check dressed up as strategy, an audit that blames them, or the large upgrade they are trying to avoid.

On money we ask ourselves, what's the easy button; any funding they can poke on; partner funding etc, and the honest answer is that the client usually pays, because a short sharply scoped assessment is carried comfortably by the value it finds. Vendor incentives help at the margins and mostly offset our cost rather than reaching the client; Salesforce pays partners for adoption after the sale, Databricks ties assessments to partner tiering, Snowflake's services fund is tier gated and AWS sometimes runs its reviews free, so we say plainly that this work is bought, not granted.

All three purchases are assembled from the same components, a baseline, a driver tree, an owner map and a cadence, so whichever door the client walks through, they finish one room closer to the Value Realisation Office whether or not they ever buy it.


# Part 4, the two moments with their own calendars: AI under scrutiny, and the deal

These last two entrances are different in kind, and the difference is worth stating. AI and deals are not conversations about a single programme; they are types of investment, and inside the method nothing about them is special, because an AI initiative and a merger still get their value framed, funded in tranches, tracked live and governed to realisation like any other investment, so there is no separate AI method here and no deal framework, and we will not build one. What earns each a page at the front is the market rather than the method; each produces its own sentence, its own buyer, its own calendar and its own vocabulary, and whoever hears the sentence in an account meeting needs to know what it means and who to call. They are lenses on the same discipline, not new machinery.

## Moment five, AI under scrutiny

The sentence is that the board is asking what the business is actually getting for its AI spend. The person saying it is the chief financial officer or the executive made accountable for AI, and the dated event is a board meeting, because the pressure described at the front of this document now has a chair and an agenda; boards are conditioning further AI funding on proof of return, and finance leaders are being asked for a number many of them cannot produce. AI is part of the conversation, but is not the whole conversation, and that sentence is the entire position; we are not selling AI strategy, models or pilots, we are helping clients realise value from AI, which is the value discipline applied to one very visible type of spend.

What this buyer cannot answer that week is basic. They cannot say what AI is costing in total, because the spend is scattered across licences, cloud consumption, embedded features and people; only about a quarter of organisations have full real time visibility of their AI costs, and the few that do are around five times more likely to show an established return. And they cannot say which initiatives are working; in Gartner's survey of finance chiefs, 62 per cent said fewer than a quarter of their AI initiatives deliver measurable benefit.

What they buy first is an AI value scan, two to four weeks, fixed fee. We inventory the spend and the initiatives, build one driver tree tying each use case to a business outcome somebody in the business will own, baseline what each really costs, and land a clear view of what to fund further, what to fix and what to stop, with the cost visibility repaired so the answer stays answerable next quarter. From there the method takes over unchanged; each surviving use case keeps its named owner and baseline, further funding is released in tranches against evidence through a sign off gate finance sits inside, which is what analysts now tell clients to demand, production proof rather than demos, the value metrics go into dashboards leaders already read, and the governance stays honest that a typical AI use case takes two to four years to reach a satisfactory return, so leading indicators carry the early reporting. The evidence on who wins with AI reads like a description of that method; across the major studies the winners share senior ownership, a few concentrated use cases rather than many pilots, redesigned ways of working and measurement from day one, which is the strongest argument that AI needs no machinery of its own. What this buyer would never buy is another AI strategy, more pilots, or anything that smells like policing the portfolio they are accountable for; the scan is theirs, built so they can walk into the board with a number and a decision. And no consultancy has yet made a named product of this conversation, so it is there to be claimed.

## Moment six, the deal

The sentence is that the deal has been signed and now the value has to show up. The buyer is the integration lead of a corporate acquisition or the operating partner behind a newly acquired portfolio company, the dated events are close, day one, the first hundred days and eventually exit, and unlike every other moment nobody here needs persuading that value matters, because their own compensation already depends on it. The whole moment is accelerating speed to value; we help clients realise value quickly from their mergers, and we help the owners of portfolios realise value more quickly from their investments.

What they cannot answer in the week after signing is whether the synergy number in the deal model is real and when it will land, and it is usually wrong; in Bain's analysis of roughly thirty one thousand companies, around 70 per cent of merging companies overestimate their synergies at announcement and only around 30 per cent of acquirers hit their targets, and realisation is lopsided, with cost synergies mostly banked inside eighteen months while revenue synergies arrive years later and far smaller than promised. Underneath that timeline sits our reason to be in the room at all; analyses that decompose synergy programmes commonly find half or more of the initiatives waiting on systems and data integration before a dollar can be counted, so the synergy timeline is usually a technology timeline, and pacing it is engineering rather than reporting.

What they buy is real work with a market vocabulary we adopt rather than rename. For a corporate deal it is synergy and value tracking stood up for the live integration, planned, forecast and actual value by workstream and date, leading adoption indicators beside lagging verified dollars, wired to the real systems rather than to slides, stood up in weeks at a fixed fee and then run as a rhythm inside the client's own integration office. For private equity it is the hundred day value plan for a portfolio company, and that window is compressing; 58 per cent of firms now put value creation resources in within the first hundred days, double the share of a year earlier, and practitioners treat an integration office empowered before day one as the strongest predictor of a good first hundred days. The moment arises when the deal does; it cannot be created, only met prepared. Where it leads is the technology integration that actually paces the synergies, and then to a destination we keep honest; a corporate buyer gets a deal value cell that lives and dies with the integration, and a private equity owner gets a standing portfolio value office, because holds now run around seven years and Bain calculates an owner now needs roughly twelve per cent annual earnings growth where five once did, because borrowing no longer does the work, so value realisation is a standing discipline of the hold, not a phase of the deal. Neither buyer gets an enterprise wide office pushed at them. What they would never buy is deal strategy from a technology consultancy, education about why value matters, or anything measured in slides.

Both pages are still the same room. The scan and the tracking are assembled from the office's own components, a baseline, a driver tree, an owner map and a cadence, wearing AI's vocabulary in one case and the deal's in the other, so these buyers also finish one room closer to a standing value discipline, whatever theirs ends up being called. And because a board date and a close date arrive on the client's calendar rather than ours, the practical consequence for us is readiness rather than campaigning; the sentence will be heard by whoever is nearest when it is said, and these two pages exist so that person knows what they just heard.


# The flagship: the Value Realisation Office

The whole practice was named in one breath at the start: setting up a vro, doing the initial business case but framing it differently so it's not just a business case that gets money in but business cases that help realise value, if not setting up the vro it could be setting up value tracking mechanisms and reporting cadence and governance to truly support value delivery. Everything since is that sentence worked all the way through, and the office is the flagship at its centre; every moment at the front opens into it and every assessment and value case is deliberately assembled from its components, yet it is never the front door, it is the room the doors lead to. It is not a stage, a phase or an end state; value gets framed before the money moves, protected while the work is running and proved after it is live, and the office is the standing function doing those three things for every investment at once, permanently.

## What the office actually is and does

In plain language, a Value Realisation Office is a small standing team that makes value somebody's actual job. It defines what value means for every material investment, using the definition in part two, naming the strategic priority each one moves rather than the technology outcome it produces. It baselines every benefit against real operating data before the change starts, because a benefit without a baseline can never be proved. It puts a named owner from the business against every number, with a date when it will be checked. It keeps the value metrics inside the dashboards leaders already review, never in a separate benefits spreadsheet, because separate tracking decays and dashboards get read. It runs the cadence; weekly the numbers stay current and the leading indicators get watched, monthly a value review ends in a real decision to continue, pause, stop or accelerate, and quarterly the executive revisits the portfolio and the cases get rebaselined. It governs the decisions that follow, releasing funding in tranches against evidence where the client's governance allows, with one senior client side owner who genuinely has the authority to stop something. And it reports value to the board rather than activity, which is the difference from a project management office; a project office tells the board the work is on time and on budget, while a value office tells it whether the business is getting what it paid for.

Where the office reports matters more than how it is drawn, and the answer is finance. The strongest offices in the market anchor there, with impact verified with finance as booked month by month, because confirmation by finance is what separates claimed value from real value, and an office that marks its own homework turns into optimistic reporting within a year; anchoring there also keeps the conversation with the chief financial officer, where this practice wants to stand.

## How one stands up

Standing one up takes three to six months and starts small on purpose. The first step is a short maturity assessment of the governance the client already runs, so the office absorbs what already works rather than duplicating it. Then it grows in stages; a handful of material investments first, with early wins reported inside the first ninety days, then the cadence, then the wider portfolio. The minimum viable version is deliberately modest; two or three people, one visible executive sponsor, a benefits register, driver trees and baselines for the investments that matter, metrics wired into the numbers leaders already read, and one monthly review ending in a real decision, and it grows only by proving value rather than by claiming territory. We build it with the client, run it together and hand it over, and the handover is the point rather than a concession; an office the client can run without us is a reference that sells the next one, and one that cannot function without us is a dependency we would eventually be paid to defend.

## The market, and what makes ours different

We did not invent this category, and its existence is good news. PwC sells the most literal version, a value realisation office run for the client as a managed service from opportunity sizing through benefits tracking to business as usual, Capgemini and Kyndryl both sell named offices and hire leaders to run them, and across the large firms the office is the anchor product, the stickiest and highest margin thing they sell. The category is proven and bought, so the only question is what makes ours different, and there are three answers. The first is where the numbers come from; the incumbent offices run on manually reported programme status, while we build on AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake and Databricks, where the consumption, adoption and pipeline data already lives, so our office gets wired to the platforms' own telemetry and the numbers arrive from the systems rather than from the programme's account of itself. We build where the numbers live, and that is a structural advantage rather than a marketing line. The second is size, because the incumbent offices are priced for very large programmes, and a standing office a mid sized organisation can actually afford is unserved. The third is the exit, because a managed service is a dependency by design and ours is designed to be handed over.

The way to be trusted with an office is to name, in the room, the ways these offices fail, since the client has usually watched one fail already. The first is tracking theatre, dashboards full of numbers that move nicely and that nobody signed up to own, the scorecard green while the value goes backwards. The second is ownership by implication, a benefit on a slide with no named owner, no baseline and no consequence, which is how value becomes everybody's aspiration and nobody's job. The third is the office as police, because an office that behaves like enforcement gets gamed or ignored, and once people feed it fiction it is worse than no office at all; this office exists to help owners hit the numbers they put their names to, so it coaches, protects the people who bring bad news early, and celebrates a smart stop as loudly as a doubling down.

## The seventh conversation

There is one client the office answers directly; they fit none of the six moments at the front, so we treat them as a seventh conversation entered here. It is the client who cannot answer the four questions in part two because they cannot yet say what value means for them, or the client who can but wants investment decisions tied to strategy rather than to whoever argued loudest in the funding round. No dated event is pulling them and no single investment will carry a value case; what they need is the discipline itself. Even then the first purchase stays small, a short fixed fee maturity assessment across the portfolio and its governance, showing where value is defined, owned and tracked today and where it is not, and the office stands up in stages from there.

This is also what the office does for us, because we need to position ourselves to have broader client conversations with more than just technology. How do we position ourselves as truly value led. Value is such a broad topic, but we need to make it easier for clients to capture value, to have the conversation on value, to realise value. The office is that ambition made structural, the client's standing capability for capturing value, the permanent home of the conversation on value, and the machinery of realising it; this is the room where the opening question gets answered continuously rather than once: what value does this investment drive for our business as a whole and how do we set ourselves up to realise it.


# How the work arrives and how the practice builds

The goal governs this whole part: setting up the client partners and internal teams well so they're able to bring me into a conversation to discuss with our clients on business value. That sentence is the whole go to market, because a practice of one does not generate its own pipeline; the pipeline is other people's ears, and work arrives through three channels, client partners in account meetings, platform partners, and engagements we already hold.

## The listening sheet

Every door has to pass one test, who cares, why do they care, what's the easy entry point, so the six moments condense into one listening sheet, the sentence, who is saying it and why now, the one line back, and the small first purchase; a client partner's job ends with the one line and a phone call.

When they hear we need a business case to get this approved, that is a sponsoring executive facing a sceptical committee with an approval date; the line back is that we can build the case that wins the approval and then actually runs the investment, inside their timetable, and the entry is a fixed fee value case of three to six weeks.

When they hear the steering committee keeps asking what value we are getting and all we can show is milestones and spend, that is a programme sponsor with a steering date; the line back is that we can wire value into the reporting the programme already runs before that meeting, and the entry is a retrofit of a few weeks paid from the programme's own budget.

When they hear I no longer trust what this programme is telling me, that is the executive above it, often the chief financial officer with a funding decision; the line back is that we can give an independent read in two to four weeks ending in continue, pause, stop or accelerate, and we only take it where Slalom is not the delivery partner under question.

When they hear we pay a great deal for this platform and I cannot see what we are getting, that is the platform owner facing a renewal or a rising consumption bill, and the business executive the platform is meant to serve; the line back is that in three weeks we will show what is locked in what they already own and how to get it out, and the entry is the fixed fee platform value assessment.

When they hear the board is asking what we are actually getting for our AI spend, that is the chief financial officer or the executive accountable for AI, with a board date; the line back is that we can hand them the answer before that date, what to fund further, what to fix and what to stop, and the entry is an AI value scan of two to four weeks.

When they hear the deal is signed and now the value has to show up, that is the integration lead or operating partner working to day one; the line back is that the value tracking can be standing before day one, wired to real systems rather than slides, and the entry is synergy tracking stood up for the live deal or a hundred day value plan for a new portfolio company.

One person cannot push six doors at once, so we lead where the demand carries a date this year, the AI moment, because boards now condition further AI funding on proof of return, and the platform moment, because renewals and rising consumption bills are landing now. The other three moments ride delivery relationships we already hold and the office is pulled rather than pushed; our relationships mostly live on the technology side while value is verified in finance, so every sponsor side entry is judged on whether it produces the finance conversation as well as the work it sells.

## The platform partners, honestly

The partners matter twice, and the honest order is doors first, money second. Their value teams build the case before the sale and stop mattering after it closes, leaving the delivery phase and the realisation after go live owned by nobody, and that gap is where we stand; the vendors also want utilisation, since adoption and consumption drive their renewals, so their account teams will walk us in at a renewal or a rising bill. The money sits at the margins; Salesforce pays partners for adoption and consumption after the sale, Databricks ties assessments to its partner tiers, Snowflake's services funding is tier gated, AWS sometimes runs its reviews free, and the only genuinely free client facing help is Microsoft's onboarding at scale and SAP's automated tooling. So incentives offset our cost rather than paying the client's bill, the default is a client paid, short, sharply scoped assessment carried by the value it finds, and what we harvest is introductions.

## The work we already hold

Most delivery accounts contain one of the six sentences this quarter, a steering pack showing spend instead of value, a consumption bill somebody winced at, an AI line the board will ask about; the retrofit funds from the programme's own budget, where we built the platform the tracking wires into its telemetry, and Slalom already sells a fixed scope health check and owns an AI value forecasting tool, so the entries extend shapes the firm has proven.

## From one person to a practice

The first engagements run in known accounts, fronted by me and picked with the client partner because a friendly account forgives a first run. Every artefact from those runs is codified, built once and reused; the value case template with driver tree, benefits register, benefit profiles and realisation plan, the platform assessment week by week, the tracking pack of dashboard wiring and a monthly value review ending in a decision, and the office starter, a maturity assessment plus a staged plan to stand one up. Reuse is the whole point; the second value case costs less than the first, a pod of two or three with a senior person fronting it can run any shape, and if every engagement stays bespoke then I am the product and the practice cannot outlive my calendar.

A young practice cannot lean on public statistics, they all need caveats, so every fixed shape engagement banks its own quantified before and after, and two numbers run the practice; paid follow on per entry engagement, because an assessment that does not convert means we have slid into the commodity health check market, and the value numbers we can quote from our own work, stated in the client's own measures rather than only in dollars. People are added on that evidence rather than a plan date; when an entry shape has converted more than once and the artefacts let somebody else front it, a second senior person joins to run one lead moment; until then pods are borrowed from delivery engagement by engagement.

What I ask of Slalom now is deliberately small; an hour with each account team to teach the listening sheet, because it only works if the sentences come back unprompted, one named account for each lead moment agreed with its client partner, agreement to publish the two entry assessments at fixed duration and fixed fee, since no competitor publishes one, and the existing AI value forecasting tool as the base for the demonstrable tracking layer the market now expects. There is no headcount ask until the conversion evidence exists. The test for all of it stays the same; it needs to be super practical and easy to have conversation with our clients on, and the question we keep asking is what do clients actually buy and want to pay for vs hypothetical.


# What is genuinely new here

Most of this practice is established discipline done properly, and that is deliberate; the discipline is decades old, every large firm sells a value office, and a proven category is what makes ours sellable. Newness therefore gets a hard test; an idea counts only if nobody else is credibly doing it, if it follows from who we already are, and if it survives scrutiny. Five pass, and each already does work earlier in this document.

## The numbers arrive from the platform, not from the programme

Every value office in the market runs on manually reported programme status, numbers typed in by the very programme being measured, so the office governs an investment on the investment's account of itself, and even the big firms' new tracking software is a container for reported numbers. Slalom builds on AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake and Databricks, where the consumption, adoption and pipeline data already lives, so our tracking wires to the platform's own telemetry and the numbers arrive from systems rather than meetings. The strategy firms cannot copy this because they do not build, and the vendors will not, because each wires only its own platform in service of its own renewal; it is also why we never say value architecture, since a competitor already owns that phrase in finance and the market has plenty of drawings and not enough plumbing. The same wiring makes the office affordable; hand collection takes people, which is partly why incumbent offices are priced for very large programmes, and a telemetry fed office gets small enough for a mid sized organisation to buy, a market simply unserved. The office described earlier rests on it; the first step is one live account where we built the platform, one benefit wired from its usage data into a dashboard the client already reads, and the before and after banked.

## The handoff the vendors leave behind

Every major vendor runs value engineering; Salesforce builds board level cases before the sale, Snowflake's engineers work inside the sales cycle, SAP has done it since 2004. They are paid to win the deal, so the case peaks on signature day and attention ends at close; delivery is measured on milestones, so the stretch where value actually arrives, delivery and the years after go live, is owned by nobody. We own that handoff as policy; wherever Slalom delivers on a platform we take over the vendor's case, baseline it, put named owners from the business against it and wire the tracking, so the document that won the money becomes the machinery that runs the investment. The gap runs wider too, because every vendor team is single vendor by construction and the strategy firms are vendor agnostic rather than vendor fluent, so nobody holds one value picture across a client's whole estate; cost got exactly that discipline years ago and value never did. The go to market part treats the vendor teams as a door, and this is why the door exists; the first step is one standard request at every platform kickoff, hand us the value case you sold this deal on.

## Three views the client cannot argue with

The platform conversation opens by reconciling three views, what the client has contracted, what has been activated, and what is actually used; the mechanic is borrowed honestly from licence management, which reconciles the same views to manage audit exposure. What is new is where we point it. Run by a firm that does not sell the licences, built from their own contracts and usage logs, and mapped against where the business is going rather than a spend line, it becomes a diagnostic the client cannot argue with, because every row is their own data, and the distance between the views is the trapped value, sized. Nobody runs it this way; the vendor's adoption dashboards exist to defend the renewal, the licence optimisation firms run it to cut spend, which reads as blame and ends in procurement, the strategy firms cannot pull the data, and no competitor publishes a fixed price offer built on it. It is week one of the platform assessment described earlier; the first step is a runbook per platform, which contract lines to read and which native usage tooling to run, proven in a friendly account before the offer is published.

## The living business case as a thing you buy

This practice began from the observation that business cases get the dollars and then go nowhere, and the market's answer has always been advice; every body of knowledge says revalidate at every gate, and the case dies anyway through the same decay, ending with nobody left who remembers what was promised. The new move is to stop advising and start selling. The install is a named piece of work with a fixed shape and a fee; it takes an approved case and turns it into a running tracking source, metrics inside the dashboards leaders already review, the case rebaselined at every gate, and a monthly review ending in a real decision. The market misses this middle; incumbents sell the diagnostic, the artefact and then the embedded office, their stickiest and highest margin product, and a small install for one investment, or a retrofit for a running programme, is on nobody's price list. That is economics rather than oversight, because a rhythm of days a month undercuts the office they would rather sell, which is exactly why it fits us. The value case work and the retrofit described earlier are this idea in different clothes; the first step costs nothing, the install goes on the price list as the second half of every value case, sold by default, so a case without one becomes the exception that needs explaining.

## Careful numbers, and the fees they earn later

This industry sells measurement using numbers that fail its own test; the transformation failure rate everyone quotes traces back to an unscientific estimate from the early nineties, the loudest AI return study of the past year rests on a small sample never peer reviewed, and the platforms' return multiples are mostly studies commissioned by the vendor whose return they calculate. A practice whose entire product is proving value cannot market itself that way, so we take the opposite position; we quote fewer numbers with the source and caveat attached, we state value in the client's own measures, and we replace the industry's statistics with before and afters banked from our own work, the discipline the practice build part already commits to. Nobody else will do this, because big round frightening numbers sell conferences and the firms that commissioned the studies cannot disown them. The position pays first as trust and later as commerce; the market is moving a meaningful share of fees onto outcome based terms and stalling because nobody can substantiate the outcome, and substantiation is exactly what careful measurement plus telemetry provides, letting us put fee at risk against verified value while others put it against activity. We say we'll deliver on time and on budget in project management, but the budget is never really the issue if you're not realising the benefits you set out to get; a fee at risk against verified benefits is the first honest version of that promise, and it goes to no client until Slalom decides it has the appetite, because pricing against outcomes is a firm level risk decision. The first step is available today; adopt the evidence standard and hold everything we publish, this document included, to it.

The spine through all five is the same; we build where the numbers live, so we can prove value where everyone else asserts it.
