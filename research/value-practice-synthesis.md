# The value practice

## The spine

1. Clients keep approving investments and cannot see, prove or steer the value coming back from them; the case that won the money goes in a drawer, delivery reports green, and nobody is tracking whether the benefits actually landed.
2. Value is the extent to which an investment moves the business towards something it already said mattered, and it only counts once someone in the business puts their name against the number and agrees when we will check it.
3. Clients tell us where they stand in six sentences, and each sentence carries its own buyer, its own date this year, and its own small first purchase.
4. When someone needs a business case to get money approved, we build a value case instead, and it keeps running long after the approval, inside the timetable they already have to hit.
5. When a programme is running with nothing to show, or when nobody trusts what it is reporting any more, we rebaseline what is left and hand the funder an honest recommendation rather than another status update.
6. When a platform a client already owns is not paying off, we show them what is locked inside it before anyone is allowed to talk about buying something new.
7. When a board is asking what the AI spend actually bought, or a deal has just been signed, the same discipline answers both, wearing the vocabulary each buyer already uses.
8. Every assessment we sell and every value case we build is deliberately assembled from the same four parts, so whichever door a client walks through, they finish one room closer to a standing Value Realisation Office.
9. The Value Realisation Office is the flagship we are building towards, wired into the platforms we already build on instead of into a programme's own account of itself, and sized for the clients the big offices have priced out.
10. We go to market through the ears of the people already sitting in these accounts, we build the practice by reusing the same artefacts on every engagement, and we prove all of it with our own numbers before we ask a client to trust anybody else's.

## The problem

Clients keep approving investments and cannot see, prove or steer the value coming back from them. The money gets approved, the platform gets bought, the programme gets delivered, and somewhere between the signature and the switch on, the value that was promised quietly stops being anyone's job. The business case wins the money and is never opened again; delivery reports green the whole way through; the team disbands the week the system goes live, and nobody is left who can say what the business actually got for it.

The loss starts before delivery begins, with roughly a quarter of a transformation's possible value lost at the target setting stage, before a single thing has been built. Then delivery succeeds while value does not; across a database of around sixteen thousand projects, only 0.5 per cent, one project in two hundred, hit cost, time and benefits together. We say we'll deliver on time and on budget in project management, but the budget is never really the issue if you're not realising the benefits you set out to get. It gets quieter still once the system is live; only 39 per cent of organisations mostly or always deliver their full project benefits, because the team disbands at go live and the benefit was never assigned to anyone still in the room when it was due. Governance reports spend and milestones instead of value, and the clearest illustration is from deals, where 83 per cent of acquisitions failed to lift shareholder returns while the integration scorecards showed green. None of this is a coin toss; a dedicated senior value owner captures 24 per cent more of the planned value than where that ownership is missing, and value is usually the only line in the whole investment that never gets one.

What makes this year different is that the question has moved into the boardroom; 66 per cent of boards now make further AI funding conditional on proof of return, and board level AI value reporting, practised by only around 4 per cent of organisations today, is expected to be standard for large enterprises by the end of this year, so plenty of clients are about to discover in front of their own board that they cannot produce the number either. We need to be solving a problem as we can't just create a solution that doesn't solve a business problem. And often we'd need to tell the client with the potential problem, because most clients recognise this the moment we say it. So every conversation this practice opens starts from one question: what value does this investment drive for our business as a whole and how do we set ourselves up to realise it.

## What we mean by value

Before we can sell value we have to say what it is, in a sentence a client can repeat back to us, and our industry has not managed that; every large firm sells value creation and defines it differently, and even the academic literature has no consensus. The definition we use is therefore a decision rather than a discovery, and saying it plainly is itself unusual in this market.

Value is the extent to which an investment moves the business towards something it already said mattered, a strategic priority rather than a technology outcome, and a system can go live perfectly, on time and on budget, and still create no value if nobody can point to the priority it was meant to move. It only counts when someone in the business owns it; they put their name against the number, agree when we will check it, and agree what happens if it does not show up, because otherwise value is a figure on a slide that belongs to nobody. Often for technology investments clients are wanting a business case to justify the investment. We need to reframe clients thinking to ensure that the investment drives business value, more than just their own dept or replacing a burning legacy platform, therefore, we need to start thinking about value cases and baselining the investment so that business value is realised beyond implementation. What strategic priorities is this investment driving as opposed to what technology outcome is it driving; allows better long term strategic decision making that will impact the business long term.

Return on investment, the industry default, fails on both counts at once; a return can be earned on something that never mattered while the business stands still, and a ratio has no owner and nobody answerable when it fails to appear. For transformation programmes, how are we actually realising the value that we set out to achieve, goes beyond roi; but what business kpis are we driving. Value is also not always money; it is defined in the client's own measures, and for some organisations the measure that matters most is safety or trust rather than a financial line.

Every definition that actually works shares the same handful of mechanics underneath all the disagreement; it is measurable, baselined, owned by a named person in the business rather than the project, time bound, tied to strategy, and about outcomes rather than outputs. Our definition is those mechanics spoken as two sentences, and the pairing beats any single alternative, because strategy without ownership is an ambition on a slide, and ownership without strategy is diligent tracking of things that may not matter.

## The six moments

We organise the front of this practice by what the client actually says, not by where an investment sits in its life, because a client partner hears one sentence in an account meeting and has to know instantly who to call and what to offer. Six sentences cover the whole practice, and each one carries a different buyer, a different date and a different first purchase.

When someone tells us they need a business case to get an investment approved, that is a sponsoring executive walking into a sceptical committee, working to the approval date, unable to say how the case will still be useful once the money has moved. What they buy is a value case for one flagship investment, three to six weeks, fixed fee, built inside their existing timetable, containing a driver tree, baselines and named owners sitting in the business rather than the project, leading into the install that keeps the case alive long after approval. They would never buy anything that slows the approval down, and they would never buy a value office that week.

When a steering committee keeps asking what value is being created and all anyone can show is milestones and spend, that is the programme sponsor or transformation lead, working to the next steering date, unable to say what the business has received or what is still coming because the benefits were never baselined. What they buy is a retrofit of a few weeks, fixed fee, funded from the programme's own budget without a new approval cycle; we rebaseline what is left, put a named owner against each benefit, and wire the numbers into the reporting the programme already runs, leading into a standing monthly value review. They would never buy anything that reads as an audit of their own business case.

When they no longer trust what a programme is telling them, the buyer sits above it, often the chief financial officer, working to the next funding decision, and what they cannot answer is the question underneath it all; what business value can this drive, how are we going to realise it post investment and implementation. What they buy is a rapid independent value diagnostic, two to four weeks, fixed fee, ending in a rebaselined case and a clear recommendation to continue, pause, stop or accelerate, and we only take this work where Slalom is not the delivery partner in question, because independence is the entire product. They would never buy more governance from the lens that already failed them.

When a client is paying a great deal for a platform and does not believe they are getting what they bought, there are two buyers, the platform owner facing a renewal or a rising bill and the business executive whose direction the platform serves, and the bill is the dated event. Are you getting the most out of any platform that you're using today is the question neither can answer, and the pattern behind it is ordinary and evidenced rather than assumed; around half of licensed software seats across the average enterprise sit unused, and more than 90 per cent of finance teams run their weekly planning in spreadsheets despite owning the platforms bought to replace them. Most organisations end up with a perfectly capable platform while the teams run the real work in spreadsheets beside it and go shopping for a new tool that does what they already own, and that happens without anyone failing; nobody had the job of getting the value out of what was already bought. You have already invested in this platform, but it's either under utilised or there are locked benefits or you could be getting so much more out of your investment, and often clients invest in these platforms and haven't even fully unlocked the capabilities they have available to them. What they buy is a platform value assessment, two to three weeks, fixed fee, reconciling what is contracted against what is activated against what is actually used, and mapping the estate against where the business is going over the next twelve to twenty four months rather than a generic checklist, because you're using this platform, your business is going in x direction, there are things we can help you unlock in this platform that will actually drive better outcomes for your business as a whole. It lands a roadmap split between what can switch on now and what needs a scoped enablement phase, which is using strategy as an enabler for our clients to unlock value in their existing platforms without having to pay millions to upgrade their platforms; we do the assessment and use it to prove the value that is locked, and then we clearly outline the roadmap to unlock trapped value in their organisations, leading into that enablement phase and into tracking wired to the platform's own telemetry. They would never buy a free health check dressed up as strategy, an audit that reads as blame, or the upgrade they are trying to avoid.

When a board is asking what the business is actually getting for its AI spend, the buyer is the chief financial officer or the executive accountable for AI, working to a board date. AI is part of the conversation, but is not the whole conversation, and that is the whole position; we are not selling AI strategy or pilots, we are helping clients realise value from AI. What they cannot answer is what the spend is actually costing or which initiatives are working. What they buy is an AI value scan, two to four weeks, fixed fee, inventorying spend and initiatives against a driver tree and giving a clear view of what to fund further, fix or stop, leading into a finance sign off gate before further funding. They would never buy another AI strategy, more pilots, or anything that smells like policing their own portfolio.

When a deal has just been signed and now the value has to show up, the buyer is the integration lead or the operating partner, working to close, day one and the first hundred days, and nobody here needs persuading that value matters because their own compensation already depends on it. The whole moment is accelerating speed to value; we help clients realise value quickly from their mergers, and we help the owners of portfolios realise value more quickly from their investments. What they cannot answer is whether the synergy number in the deal model is real and when it will land; around 70 per cent of acquirers overestimate their synergies at announcement and only around 30 per cent hit their targets, and the shortfall is usually a technology timeline in disguise. What they buy is synergy and value tracking stood up for a live integration, or a hundred day value plan for a newly acquired portfolio company, wired to real systems rather than slides, leading into the technology integration that actually paces the synergies and then to a destination that differs by buyer; a corporate acquirer gets a deal value cell that lives and dies with the integration, while a private equity owner gets a standing portfolio value office, because holds now average around seven years and the growth needed to make the returns work has roughly doubled since multiple expansion stopped doing the work. They would never buy deal strategy from a technology consultancy, or education about why value matters.

## What we sell

It needs to be super practical and easy to have conversation with our clients on, so the practice packages its work by shape rather than by moment, because a shape can be staffed, priced, reused and turned into a reference, and the same shape recurs across several moments.

The first shape is the assessment: short, sharply scoped, fixed length and fixed fee, published rather than hidden behind a sales conversation, staffed by a pod of two or three people with a senior person fronting it. The family covers the platform value assessment, the AI value scan, the deal value check, and a value maturity assessment that also doubles as the first step of standing up an office.

The second shape is the value case and the install that makes it live. Business cases are just set up to get the dollars and then go nowhere and aren't living, breathing documents, how do we actually turn those business cases into living and breathing baseline and metric tracking sources that are used throughout the engagement. So the case comes with an install: the metrics move inside the dashboards leaders already review, a monthly value review ends in a real decision, and the case is rebaselined at every gate. This shape carries the one artefact family the whole practice reuses, a benefits register, benefit profiles with a named owner in the business, a driver tree and a realisation plan.

The third shape is the office itself, sized properly in the next part; every assessment and every value case is deliberately built from its components, so the client finishes closer to it whether or not they ever buy it.

On money we ask ourselves what's the easy button, any funding they can poke on, partner funding etc, and the honest answer is that the default is the client pays, because a short sharply scoped assessment is carried comfortably by the value it finds. Vendor incentives help at the margins but mostly offset our own cost rather than reaching the client, so we say plainly that this work is bought, not granted.

## What we actually do, every time

It's more than just reframing it to a value case, it's how do we ensure that this is driving business value, how do we set it up so that we can show the value delivered and how do we ensure the value is delivered, reported. Four things follow from that, and we do them on every investment regardless of what kind it is or where it sits in its life.

We frame the value, building a driver tree down from the board level outcome to the operational measures a manager can act on weekly, baselined against real operating data before anything changes, with a named owner sitting in the business rather than in the project. We fund it properly, releasing money in tranches against evidence where the client's governance allows it. We track it live, putting the metrics inside the dashboards leaders already review rather than a separate spreadsheet that decays, and wiring them to the platform's own telemetry wherever we built it. And we govern it to realisation, with a fixed cadence, a real decision at the end of it, and one senior owner who genuinely has the authority to stop something.

These four things run across the whole life of an investment, from before the money moves until long after the delivery team has disbanded, which is why one practice can serve six different conversations; value gets framed before the money moves, protected while the work is running, and proved after it is live, and a standing office does all three for every investment at once, permanently, rather than being the last stop on a line.

We name the failure modes in front of the client, because naming them is what makes this credible: benefit tracking theatre, where the dashboard moves nicely and nobody signed up to own it; post project amnesia, where the team disbands and the benefit goes with it; the wallpaper business case nobody has opened since approval; ownership by implication, a benefit on a slide with no name against it; and a value office that behaves like a police force instead of a coach.

And every assessment we sell and every value case we build is deliberately assembled out of the same four parts, a baseline, a driver tree, an owner map and a cadence, so every engagement leaves the client one room closer to the office whether they buy it or not.

## The Value Realisation Office

Setting up a vro, doing the initial business case but framing it differently so it's not just a business case that gets money in but business cases that help realise value, if not setting up the vro it could be setting up value tracking mechanisms and reporting cadence and governance to truly support value delivery, is the sentence this whole practice was named from, and the office is the flagship it points to. It is the standing version of the same four things we do on every investment, done for all of them at once, permanently, and every moment at the front and every shape we sell is deliberately built to lead into it.

A Value Realisation Office is a small standing team that makes value somebody's actual job across the whole portfolio, reporting to the board rather than a steering committee, and anchored to finance, because confirmation by finance is what separates claimed value from real value. Standing one up takes three to six months and starts small on purpose, with early wins reported inside the first ninety days, growing only by proving value rather than by claiming territory. We build it with the client, run it together, and hand it over, and the handover is the point rather than a concession, because an office the client can run is a reference and an office they depend on is a liability.

Every large firm sells a version of this category, which is good news, because it means the category is proven and bought. What makes ours different is where the numbers come from; the incumbent offices run on manually reported programme status, while we sit inside the AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake and Databricks build, where the consumption, adoption and pipeline data already lives, so our office wires to the platforms' own telemetry instead of a programme's account of itself. It is also why we take on the handoff the vendors leave behind; their own value teams build the case before the sale and stop mattering after it closes, and every one of them is single vendor by construction, while we can hold one value picture across a client's whole estate. And because hand collected reporting takes people, the incumbent offices are priced for very large programmes, while a telemetry fed office can be small enough for a mid sized organisation to actually afford, a market nobody else is serving.

There is one client this office answers directly even though they fit none of the six moments; this is the seventh conversation, entered directly. It is the client who cannot say what value means for them, or who wants investment decisions tied to strategy rather than to whoever argued loudest in the funding round, and even there the first purchase stays small, a short maturity assessment across the portfolio, with the office standing up in stages from that. We need to position ourselves to have broader client conversations with more than just technology. How do we position ourselves as truly value led. Value is such a broad topic, but we need to make it easier for clients to capture value, to have the conversation on value, to realise value, and the office is that ambition made structural, the room where the opening question gets answered continuously rather than once: what value does this investment drive for our business as a whole and how do we set ourselves up to realise it.

## Going to market

Setting up the client partners and internal teams well so they're able to bring me into a conversation to discuss with our clients on business value is the whole go to market, because a practice of one person does not generate its own pipeline; the pipeline is other people's ears. The six moments become one listening sheet a client partner can actually use: the sentence, who is saying it and why now, the line back, and the small first purchase, so their job ends with one line and a phone call.

One person cannot push six doors at once, so we lead with the two moments where the demand already carries a date this year, the AI moment and the platform moment, because boards are asking now and renewals and rising bills are landing now. The other moments ride the delivery relationships we already hold, and the office is pulled rather than pushed.

The vendor partners matter twice, and the honest order is doors before money. Their value teams build the case before the sale and stop mattering after close, which leaves delivery and the years after go live owned by nobody, and that gap is where we stand; the vendors also want utilisation, since adoption drives their own renewals, so their account teams walk us into a renewal or a rising bill. Salesforce pays for adoption after the sale, Databricks ties assessments to partner tiering, Snowflake gates its services fund by tier, and AWS sometimes runs a well architected review free; the money sits at the margins in every case, offsetting our cost rather than paying the client's bill, so the honest default stays a client paid, fixed fee assessment.

Our relationships mostly live on the technology side while value gets verified in finance, so moments one, two and four enter through relationships we already hold, while moments three and five and the office itself walk the conversation to the chief financial officer; every sponsor side engagement is judged on whether it produces that finance conversation, not only on the work it sells.

## Building the practice

The practice builds by reusing the same artefacts on every engagement: the value case template, the platform assessment run sheet, the tracking pack that wires dashboards to a monthly review, and the office starter kit of a maturity assessment plus a staged stand up plan. Reuse is the whole point; the second value case costs less than the first, a pod of two or three people with a senior person fronting it can run any shape, and if every engagement stays bespoke then I am the product and the practice cannot outlive my calendar.

A young practice cannot lean on public statistics, since they all need caveats, so every fixed shape engagement banks its own quantified before and after, and those references are what eventually make the office sellable. What do clients actually buy and want to pay for vs hypothetical is the question that decides what gets built next, not a plan date; a second senior person joins to run a lead moment once an entry shape has converted more than once and the artefacts let somebody else front it.

The same evidence discipline points somewhere further out. The market is moving a meaningful share of fees onto outcome based terms and stalling because nobody can substantiate the outcome, and telemetry access is what makes substantiation possible; a fee genuinely at risk against verified value, rather than against activity, is the honest version of the promise this whole practice replaces. We say we'll deliver on time and on budget in project management, but the budget is never really the issue if you're not realising the benefits you set out to get, and fee at risk against a number finance has confirmed is what makes that promise real instead of aspirational. It goes to no client until Slalom decides it has the appetite for it, because pricing against outcomes is a firm level decision; the first step available today is holding everything we publish, this document included, to the same standard we are asking clients to run their investments to.

## The first ninety days

What I am asking for now is deliberately small. An hour with each account team to teach the listening sheet, because it only works if the sentences come back unprompted; one named account for each lead moment, agreed with its client partner; agreement to publish the two entry assessments at a fixed duration and fixed fee, since no competitor publishes one; and the existing AI value forecasting tool as the base for the tracking layer the market now expects. There is no headcount ask until the conversion evidence exists. In the first ninety days the first platform assessment and the first AI value scan should both be running in known accounts, fronted by me and picked with the client partner because a friendly account forgives a first run, with every artefact from those two engagements codified for reuse and one quantified before and after banked from each.
