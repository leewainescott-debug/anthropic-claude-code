# The value practice

## The spine

1. Clients keep approving investments and cannot see, prove or steer the value coming back from them; the case goes in a drawer, delivery reports green, and nobody tracks whether the benefits landed.
2. Value is the extent to which an investment moves the business towards something it already said mattered, and it only counts once someone in the business owns the number and agrees when we will check it.
3. Clients tell us where they stand in six sentences, each carrying its own buyer, date and first purchase.
4. When someone needs a business case approved, we build a value case instead, one that keeps running long after approval.
5. When a programme has nothing to show, or nobody trusts what it is reporting, we rebaseline what is left and give the funder an honest recommendation.
6. When a client already owns a platform that is under utilised, we show them what is locked inside it before they buy something new.
7. When a board asks what its AI spend bought, or a deal has just closed, the same discipline answers both, in the buyer's own language.
8. Every assessment and every value case we build is deliberately assembled from the same four parts, so each door leads one room closer to a standing Value Realisation Office.
9. The office is the flagship we are building towards, wired into the platforms we build on rather than a programme's own account of itself, and sized for clients the big offices have priced out.
10. We go to market through the ears of people already in these accounts, build the practice by reusing the same artefacts on every engagement, and prove it with our own numbers first.

## The problem

Clients keep approving investments and cannot see, prove or steer the value coming back from them; the case that won the money is never opened again, delivery reports green throughout, the team disbands at go live, and nobody can say what the business actually got.

Roughly a quarter of a transformation's value is lost at the business case stage before anything is built, and only 0.5 per cent of projects in one large database, one in two hundred, ever hit cost, time and benefits together. We say we'll deliver on time and on budget in project management, but the budget is never really the issue if you're not realising the benefits you set out to get. Only 39 per cent of organisations mostly or always deliver their full benefits, and 83 per cent of acquisitions have shown green scorecards while shareholder returns fell.

This year the question moved into the boardroom; 66 per cent of boards now make further AI funding conditional on proof of return, and board level AI value reporting, at only around 4 per cent of organisations today, is expected to be standard by year end. We need to be solving a problem as we can't just create a solution that doesn't solve a business problem. And often we'd need to tell the client with the potential problem. So every conversation this practice opens starts from one question: what value does this investment drive for our business as a whole and how do we set ourselves up to realise it.

## What we mean by value

Before we can sell value we have to say what it is, in a sentence a client can repeat back, and our industry has not managed that; every large firm sells value creation and defines it differently, and even the academic literature has no consensus. The definition we use is therefore a decision rather than a discovery.

Value is the extent to which an investment moves the business towards something it already said mattered, a strategic priority rather than a technology outcome, and it only counts when someone in the business owns it, puts their name against the number, agrees when we will check it, and agrees what happens if it does not show up. Often for technology investments clients are wanting a business case to justify the investment. We need to reframe clients thinking to ensure that the investment drives business value, more than just their own dept or replacing a burning legacy platform, therefore, we need to start thinking about value cases and baselining the investment so that business value is realised beyond implementation. What strategic priorities is this investment driving as opposed to what technology outcome is it driving; allows better long term strategic decision making that will impact the business long term.

Return on investment fails the same way twice; a return can be earned on something that never mattered, and a ratio has no owner to answer when it fails to appear. For transformation programmes, how are we actually realising the value that we set out to achieve, goes beyond roi; but what business kpis are we driving. Value is also not always money; it is defined in the client's own measures.

Every definition that actually works shares the same mechanics; measurable, baselined, owned by a named person in the business rather than the project, time bound, tied to strategy, and about outcomes rather than outputs, which is what our two sentences say, and why the pairing beats any single alternative.

## The six moments

We organise the front of this practice by what the client says, not by where an investment sits in its life; each of six sentences carries a different buyer, date and first purchase.

When someone needs a business case approved, that is a sponsoring executive facing a sceptical committee, unable to say how the case stays useful once the money has moved. They buy a value case for one flagship investment, three to six weeks, fixed fee, inside their timetable, with a driver tree, baselines and named owners in the business, leading into the install that keeps it alive after approval. They would never buy anything that slows the approval down, or a value office that week.

When a steering committee keeps asking what value is being created and all anyone can show is milestones and spend, that is the programme sponsor, unable to say what the business has received because the benefits were never baselined. They buy a retrofit of a few weeks, fixed fee, funded from the programme's own budget; we rebaseline what is left, name an owner for each benefit, and wire the numbers into the reporting the programme already runs, leading into a standing monthly review. They would never buy an audit of their own case.

When they no longer trust what a programme is telling them, the buyer sits above it, often the chief financial officer; what business value can this drive, how are we going to realise it post investment and implementation, is what they cannot answer. They buy a rapid independent value diagnostic, two to four weeks, fixed fee, ending in a rebaselined case and a recommendation to continue, pause, stop or accelerate, and we only take this work where Slalom is not the delivery partner in question, because independence is the entire product. They would never buy more governance from the lens that already failed them.

When a client pays a great deal for a platform and does not believe they are getting what they bought, there are two buyers, the platform owner facing a renewal or a rising bill and the executive whose direction the platform serves. Are you getting the most out of any platform that you're using today is what neither can answer, and the pattern is ordinary and evidenced; around half of licensed software seats sit unused across the average enterprise, and more than 90 per cent of finance teams run weekly planning in spreadsheets despite owning the platforms bought to replace them. An organisation can own a perfectly capable platform while the teams work around it in spreadsheets and go shopping for a new tool that does what they already own. You have already invested in this platform, but it's either under utilised or there are locked benefits or you could be getting so much more out of your investment, and often clients invest in these platforms and haven't even fully unlocked the capabilities they have available to them. They buy a platform value assessment, two to three weeks, fixed fee, reconciling what is contracted, activated and actually used, and mapping the estate against where the business is going over the next twelve to twenty four months, because you're using this platform, your business is going in x direction, there are things we can help you unlock in this platform that will actually drive better outcomes for your business as a whole. This is using strategy as an enabler for our clients to unlock value in their existing platforms without having to pay millions to upgrade their platforms; we do the assessment and use it to prove the value that is locked, and then we clearly outline the roadmap to unlock trapped value in their organisations. They would never buy a free health check dressed up as strategy, an audit that reads as blame, or the upgrade they are trying to avoid.

When a board is asking what the business is actually getting for its AI spend, the buyer is the chief financial officer or the executive accountable for AI. AI is part of the conversation, but is not the whole conversation; we are not selling AI strategy or pilots, we are helping clients realise value from AI. They cannot answer what the spend is costing or which initiatives are working. They buy an AI value scan, two to four weeks, fixed fee, inventorying spend against a driver tree and giving a clear view of what to fund, fix or stop, leading into a finance sign off gate. They would never buy another AI strategy, more pilots, or anything that smells like policing their own portfolio.

When a deal has just been signed and now the value has to show up, the buyer is the integration lead or the operating partner, and their own compensation already depends on it. The whole moment is accelerating speed to value; we help clients realise value quickly from their mergers, and we help the owners of portfolios realise value more quickly from their investments. They cannot answer whether the synergy number is real and when it will land; around 70 per cent of acquirers overestimate their synergies at announcement and only around 30 per cent hit their targets. They buy synergy and value tracking stood up for a live integration, or a hundred day value plan for a newly acquired portfolio company, wired to real systems rather than slides, then a destination that differs by buyer; a corporate acquirer gets a deal value cell that lives and dies with the integration, while a private equity owner gets a standing portfolio value office, because holds now average around seven years. They would never buy deal strategy from a technology consultancy, or education about why value matters.

## What we sell

It needs to be super practical and easy to have conversation with our clients on, so the practice packages its work by shape rather than by moment, because a shape can be staffed, priced, reused and turned into a reference.

The first shape is the assessment: short, sharply scoped, fixed length and fixed fee, staffed by a pod of two or three with a senior person fronting it. The family covers the platform value assessment, the AI value scan, the deal value check, and a value maturity assessment that doubles as the first step of standing up an office.

The second shape is the value case and the install that makes it live. Business cases are just set up to get the dollars and then go nowhere and aren't living, breathing documents, how do we actually turn those business cases into living and breathing baseline and metric tracking sources that are used throughout the engagement. The install puts the metrics inside the dashboards leaders already review, ends each month in a real decision, and rebaselines the case at every gate, building the artefact family the whole practice reuses.

The third shape is the office itself, sized properly in the next part.

On money we ask what's the easy button, any funding they can poke on, partner funding etc, and the honest answer is that the default is the client pays, because a short sharply scoped assessment is carried comfortably by the value it finds; vendor incentives help at the margins but mostly offset our own cost, so this work is bought, not granted.

## What we actually do, every time

It's more than just reframing it to a value case, it's how do we ensure that this is driving business value, how do we set it up so that we can show the value delivered and how do we ensure the value is delivered, reported. Four things follow, done on every investment regardless of its kind or where it sits in its life.

We frame the value, building a driver tree down from the board level outcome to the operational measures a manager can act on weekly, baselined against real data before anything changes, with a named owner in the business. We fund it properly, releasing money in tranches against evidence where governance allows it. We track it live, putting the metrics inside the dashboards leaders already review rather than a spreadsheet that decays, wiring them to the platform's own usage data wherever we built it. And we govern it to realisation, with a fixed cadence, a real decision at the end, and one senior owner with genuine authority to stop something.

These four things run across the whole life of an investment, from before the money moves until long after the delivery team has disbanded, which is why one practice serves six conversations; value gets framed early, protected while the work runs, and proved after it is live, and a standing office does all three at once, permanently.

We name the failure modes in front of the client, because naming them is what makes this credible: benefit tracking theatre, where the dashboard moves nicely and nobody signed up to own it; post project amnesia, where the team disbands and the benefit goes with it; the wallpaper business case nobody has opened since approval; ownership by implication, a benefit with no name against it; and a value office that behaves like a police force instead of a coach.

Every assessment and value case we build is deliberately assembled out of the same four parts, a baseline, a driver tree, an owner map and a cadence, so every engagement leaves the client one room closer to the office whether they buy it or not.

## The Value Realisation Office

Setting up a vro, doing the initial business case but framing it differently so it's not just a business case that gets money in but business cases that help realise value, if not setting up the vro it could be setting up value tracking mechanisms and reporting cadence and governance to truly support value delivery, is the sentence this whole practice was named from, and the office is the flagship it points to; every moment and every shape we sell is built to lead into it.

A Value Realisation Office is a small standing team that makes value somebody's actual job across the whole portfolio, reporting to the board and anchored to finance, because finance confirmation is what separates claimed value from real value. Standing one up takes three to six months, starting small, with early wins inside the first ninety days, growing only by proving value rather than by claiming territory. We build it with the client, run it together, and hand it over, because an office the client can run is a reference and an office they depend on is a liability.

Every large firm sells a version of this category, which is good news, because the category is proven and bought. What makes ours different is where the numbers come from; the incumbent offices run on manually reported programme status, while we sit inside the AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake and Databricks build, so our office wires to the platforms' own usage data instead of a programme's account of itself. It is also why we take on the handoff the vendors leave behind; their value teams build the case before the sale and stop mattering after it closes, and every one is single vendor, while we can hold one value picture across a client's whole estate. And because hand collected reporting takes people, the incumbent offices are priced for very large programmes, while an office fed by the platform's own usage data can be small enough for a mid sized organisation to actually afford.

There is one client this office answers directly though they fit none of the six moments; this is the seventh conversation, the one who cannot say what value means for them or who wants investment decisions tied to strategy rather than whoever argued loudest in the funding round, and even there the first purchase stays small, a maturity assessment across the portfolio, building up in stages. We need to position ourselves to have broader client conversations with more than just technology. How do we position ourselves as truly value led. Value is such a broad topic, but we need to make it easier for clients to capture value, to have the conversation on value, to realise value, and the office is that ambition made structural: what value does this investment drive for our business as a whole and how do we set ourselves up to realise it.

## Going to market

Setting up the client partners and internal teams well so they're able to bring me into a conversation to discuss with our clients on business value is the whole go to market, because a practice of one person does not generate its own pipeline. The six moments become one listening sheet: the sentence, who is saying it and why now, the line back, and the small first purchase, so a client partner's job ends with one line and a phone call.

One person cannot push six doors at once, so we lead with the AI moment and the platform moment, because the demand there already carries a date this year; boards are asking now, and renewals and rising bills are landing now. The other moments ride the delivery relationships we already hold, and the office is pulled rather than pushed.

The vendor partners matter twice, and the honest order is doors before money. Their value teams build the case before the sale and stop mattering after close, leaving delivery and the years after go live owned by nobody, and that gap is where we stand. Salesforce pays for adoption after the sale, Databricks ties assessments to tiering, Snowflake gates its fund by tier, and AWS sometimes runs a review free, but the money sits at the margins everywhere, offsetting our cost rather than the client's bill.

Our relationships mostly live on the technology side while value gets verified in finance, so moments one, two and four enter through relationships we already hold, while moments three and five and the office itself walk the conversation to the chief financial officer; every sponsor side engagement is judged on whether it produces that finance conversation, not only on the work it sells.

## Building the practice

The practice builds by reusing the same artefacts on every engagement: the value case template, the platform assessment run sheet, the tracking pack, and the office starter kit of a maturity assessment plus a staged stand up plan. Reuse is the whole point; a pod of two or three with a senior person fronting it can run any shape, and if every engagement stays bespoke then I am the product and the practice cannot outlive my calendar.

A young practice cannot lean on public statistics, since they all need caveats, so every fixed shape engagement banks its own quantified before and after, and those references are what eventually make the office sellable. What do clients actually buy and want to pay for vs hypothetical decides what gets built next; a second senior person joins to run a lead moment once an entry shape has converted more than once.

The same evidence discipline points further out. The market is moving fees onto outcome based terms and stalling because nobody can substantiate the outcome, and access to the platform's own usage data is what makes substantiation possible; a fee genuinely at risk against verified value is the honest version of the promise this practice replaces. We say we'll deliver on time and on budget in project management, but the budget is never really the issue if you're not realising the benefits you set out to get. It goes to no client until Slalom decides it has the appetite, because pricing against outcomes is a firm level decision; the first step today is holding everything we publish, this document included, to that same standard.

## The first ninety days

What I am asking for now is deliberately small: an hour with each account team to teach the listening sheet, because it only works if the sentences come back unprompted; one named account for each lead moment, agreed with its client partner; agreement to publish the two entry assessments at a fixed duration and fixed fee, since no competitor publishes one; and the existing AI value forecasting tool as the base for the tracking layer the market now expects. There is no headcount ask until the conversion evidence exists. In the first ninety days the platform assessment and the AI value scan should both be running in known accounts, fronted by me, with every artefact codified for reuse and a quantified before and after banked from each.
