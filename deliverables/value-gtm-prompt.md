### 2. How value is defined

Financial value is the only dimension every firm and discipline names; beyond that the field has not settled on one definition. McKinsey defines value as economic profit, the cash a business generates above the cost of capital employed. BCG decomposes value into total shareholder return: growth, margin and the valuation multiple. Bain works from a customer value signature, using its thirty element Elements of Value framework alongside Net Promoter Score, and treats value as the change actually landing rather than being designed on paper. Deloitte's Enterprise Value Map cascades revenue growth, operating margin and asset efficiency through the business, and its term value architecture describes an outcome broken into a value tree and objectives and key results, a detail that reached this research secondhand and needs verification. EY's Long Term Value framework covers customer, people, societal and financial value, treating the financial result as the outcome the other three produce. Accenture's 360 Value framework covers financial, client, talent, inclusion, experience and sustainability value, the most symmetric framework in the market. PwC runs two unreconciled practices, one that monetises everything including externalities and one, from Strategy&, that uses a classic value bridge instead, so even inside one firm the definition has not been settled. KPMG frames value as an enterprise value uplift achieved through five levers, and its own November 2025 report admits value creation is a concept often discussed yet rarely defined with precision, a usable line to quote back to a client. Partners in Performance ties value to performance based fees, so value is whatever it will put a contingent fee against.

Outside the big firms, the Project Management Institute defines a benefit as the value created for a sponsor and insists the improvement has to be measurable. Jenner's Managing Benefits defines value as the satisfaction of needs relative to the resources used, separating benefits into cashable and non cashable. The UK Green Book defines value for money as a balanced judgement rather than only monetised impacts, the formal counter argument to monetising everything. Cranfield's Benefits Dependency Network separates the means, the technology, from the ways, the business change, from the ends, the benefits themselves. Value engineering, from Miles in 1947, defines value as function divided by cost. Vendors run their own practices too: SAP's value engineering group builds cases from a value driven strategy, ServiceNow's Now Value moves through envision, create, validate and champion, Salesforce has a Business Value Services function reconstructed from job postings, and Gartner has a Business Value Model linking metrics back to the financial statements.

The dimensions that keep recurring are financial, customer or experience, people, operational or execution, risk, strategic or positional, and societal or public value, and only the benefits discipline and the public sector treat the non financial dimensions as equal to the financial one.

Every usable definition shares six working parts: it has to be measurable, baselined, owned by a named beneficiary in the business rather than the project team, time bound and checked on a fixed schedule after go live, tied to strategy, and framed as an outcome rather than an output.

The field genuinely disagrees on four forks. The first is whether value belongs to shareholders first or is inclusive of every stakeholder. The second is whether to monetise everything into one number or keep a scorecard in each measure's own native units, a decision best made per client. The third is whether to measure the value of the technology itself, the vendor's frame, or the value to the business, the strategy frame, and Cranfield's network is the structural fix since it separates means, ways and ends. The fourth is whether to define value as a ratio, benefit over resources, or as a delta, the surplus above the cost of capital, and most boards already think in one of these terms, so the choice should follow whichever language the board already speaks.

A 2024 review in the Journal of Business Ethics covering 288 articles on social value found five distinct approaches and concluded no consensus exists, evidence this question is not settled in the academic literature either (confidence B).

### 3. The market for value work

Every serious player sells the same three layer product shape: a diagnostic that baselines the client's value position against peers, such as McKinsey's Value Assurance 360 Assessment or SAP's customer value assessment; a value artefact, a value tree, value bridge or value map that decomposes a financial north star into initiatives and key performance indicators, such as Bain's value bridge or Deloitte's Enterprise Value Map; and an embedded office, an ongoing governance function that tracks forecast against actual, examples being Bain's Results Delivery Office, PwC's Value Realization Managed Services, Capgemini's value realisation office, McKinsey's Transformation Office and Accenture's Transformation Office. Every firm shares the same enemy, value leakage, and the same conviction that adoption and behaviour change, more than the analytical case, are where things actually fail.

By firm: McKinsey sells Value Assurance and a Transformation Office, with around 25 per cent of its fees now outcomes based and value assurance meaning finance embedded tracking against target (confidence B). BCG sells an AI Powered Transformation Office launched in 2026, defines value as total shareholder return, and states plainly that AI does not create value by default. Bain sells Results Delivery, proven by change managers who deliver more than 86 per cent of promised results against 43 per cent for the worst, across more than three hundred programmes (confidence B). Deloitte uses the term value architecture in a capital allocation context, with impact verified as booked monthly with finance, a detail needing verification since it reached the research secondhand (confidence C). PwC sells Value Realization Managed Services, the most literal buy a value office as a service offer on the market. EY sells Long Term Value on four pillars and is experimenting with outcomes based AI billing. Accenture sells Value and Benefits Realisation Services on a platform called Momentum, dedicated to the why rather than the how of technology. KPMG sells Elevate, more than three hundred value based analytical modules. Partners in Performance charges contingent fees, putting its money where its claims are. Kyndryl, Capgemini and Fujitsu all run named value realisation offices, and Kyndryl calls them air traffic controllers of transformation. Value architecture itself is generic vocabulary that nobody owns.

The whitespace, meaning where a smaller, platform native player can win, sits in six places. Value plumbing alongside value architecture is the strongest: MBB value offices run on manually reported programme status, while a firm inside the AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake and Databricks build itself can wire value tracking directly into the platforms' own usage data, and nothing in the research found a competitor doing this well. The second is the vendor handoff gap, covered in the next section. The third is working across platforms, since every vendor's own value team is single vendor and the MBB firms are vendor agnostic without being vendor fluent. The fourth is the mid market, since PwC, Capgemini and McKinsey all price for mega programmes. The fifth is a public, fixed price entry point, since no competitor publishes one and a simple fixed number of days for a fixed fee would be a differentiator on its own. The sixth is verifiable outcome pricing, since the blocker across the market is proof, and a firm with direct access to the platforms' own usage data is structurally better placed to stand behind a fee at risk model than a strategy firm is.

### 4. The vendor value engineering teams

Every major platform runs its own value function, and every one of them is motivated by the sale rather than by what happens afterward. SAP has run Value Engineering since 2004, ServiceNow calls its version Now Value plus Impact, Salesforce runs Business Value Services, and Snowflake has its own Value Engineering team. All four exist to justify that vendor's own deal, all four are single vendor by design, and all four hand off once the deal closes, leaving nobody responsible for whether the value promised at signature actually shows up afterward. Databricks has no publicly named equivalent, itself a gap in the market.

Beneath these sits a layer of adoption and health tools that tells the same story from a different angle. Salesforce offers Optimizer, a free self service scan, and free partner health checks that work off a checklist and function mainly as lead generation, while its paid Signature and Premier Success Plans produce a Customer Success Score built from product adoption, expertise and technical health. ServiceNow's HealthScan is delivered by consultants, and its Impact tiers are priced as a percentage of the subscription and paid for by the client, with hidden costs flagged by independent advisors (confidence C); across these tools, the pattern is that projects finish on deadline and within budget, but afterward a disenchantment sets in because the value is not delivering. Microsoft's FastTrack is the cleanest exception in the market, genuinely free for any customer with 150 or more seats for the life of the subscription.

The honest funding picture follows directly from this. Beyond a small number of free, automated tools, mainly FastTrack and SAP's automated tooling, the client pays for a short, sharply scoped, human delivered assessment by default, and partner incentive funds exist mainly to offset the partner's own cost of delivery rather than to function as a grant handed to the client. This is the exact gap a platform native realisation practice should own: the vendor's own team builds the pre sale case and proves value at signature, then disappears, leaving the entire realisation phase, meaning what happens during delivery and for years after go live, open to whoever is willing to own it, and nobody in the research is doing that across more than one vendor at once.

### 5. The platform reality

The underuse evidence is broad and consistent. The average enterprise runs 305 SaaS applications, spends 55 million dollars a year on SaaS, and leaves 46 per cent of its licenses unused, wasting roughly 19.8 million dollars per organisation per year (Zylo, 2025 to 2026, confidence A). Cloud waste rose to 29 per cent of spend in 2026, the first increase in five years (Flexera, 2026, confidence A), and idle or oversized compute typically eats up more than 30 to 40 per cent of Snowflake and Databricks spend specifically, a blended estimate needing verification per client (confidence C). Only 42 per cent of companies fully achieve the cloud value they expected, only 5 points higher than in 2020 (Accenture, January 2025, confidence A). On Salesforce, only 33 per cent of AI initiatives meet the return expected of them, and only 26 per cent of customers say most of their customer data actually sits in Salesforce at all (IBM Institute for Business Value, confidence A). More than 90 per cent of finance practitioners use spreadsheets weekly for planning even though they already own a finance platform, direct evidence that spreadsheets sit alongside the platform rather than replacing it (Association for Financial Professionals, 2025, confidence A), which is exactly why Gartner argues that application rationalisation has to move beyond cost savings into a strategy conversation.

The mechanic that diagnoses this well is to reconcile three views of a platform: what was contracted, what was deployed or activated, and what is actually used. The gaps between those views cut both ways, creating audit exposure on one side and reclaimable value on the other, and naming all three explicitly turns a vague sense of waste into a defensible finding.

A grounded engagement runs two to three weeks. Week one reconciles what was contracted against what was activated against what is used, runs the vendor's own usage tooling as a baseline, and splits sessions between platform admins and business users to see where workarounds and spreadsheets sit next to the platform and why. Week two maps the findings to business direction: an executive workshop covers where the business is going over the next 12 to 24 months, and the capability inventory gets checked against that direction rather than a generic checklist, so everything gets prioritised by relevance to a real outcome. Week three delivers the roadmap, tying every item to a named business outcome, sorting it into turn on now or needs a scoped enablement phase, the natural point the engagement converts into further work, and closing with a lightweight measurement cadence so the client ends up with an ongoing operating discipline rather than a one time report.

The honest funding picture differs by partner and should be checked as current before it is quoted to a client. AWS migration acceleration assessment funding runs at about 5 per cent of projected annual recurring revenue, with 15 to 25 per cent in credits at scale, and Well-Architected reviews are free. Snowflake's Services Funds Program runs at 3 to 5 per cent and LiftOff funds foundation work, while its Well-Architected tool is free. Databricks introduced a Partner Well-Architected Framework in February 2026 tying assessments to partner tiering and go to market incentives. Salesforce's FY27 program introduces consumption funds that reward adoption, the natural funding line for helping a client unlock what it already bought, though its partner activation program pays partners rather than clients directly. Funded vehicles often cannot be stacked but can be chained, a proof of concept first and a migration assessment behind it. The plain read is that the client pays for a short, sharply scoped assessment by default; Microsoft's FastTrack, free for any customer with 150 or more seats, and SAP's automated discovery tooling are the only two genuinely free mechanisms found in this research.

A commodity engagement looks like a licence audit or a free health check, running off a checklist and existing mainly as lead generation. A strategy led engagement starts from the outcome logic of the organisation's own strategy, talks about value leakage rather than wasted spend, prioritises by relevance to business outcomes rather than licence dollars, and ends in an ongoing operating cadence rather than a one time report. Independence is the credibility angle that matters most here, since a firm that does not sell licences itself has less reason to shade the finding.

### 7. What a value realisation office actually does

A value realisation office works through ten mechanisms, and all ten matter more than any single tool or dashboard. Every benefit needs one named owner inside the operating business, never the project team, since the project team disbands and a disbanded owner cannot keep owning anything; it needs to be baselined before the change starts, against real operating data; and a value driver tree should break the headline number into leading indicators a manager can act on week by week. Value metrics need to live inside the dashboards leaders already review rather than a separate benefits spreadsheet, which always decays, and the business case should be rebaselined at every stage gate so each gate revalidates the benefits and not only the budget. Funding should release in stages tied to evidenced milestones, the way venture funding works, and one senior accountable owner should hold explicit authority to kill or pause the initiative, following the UK public sector's senior responsible owner model. A review, run a few weeks to a few months after go live, should check the benefit actually realised against the original target, and smart kills should be celebrated and bad news protected, because sunk cost bias otherwise keeps dead initiatives alive. A fixed cadence works best across all of it: a weekly delivery forum, a monthly value review ending in a decision to go, hold, kill or accelerate, and a quarterly review with executives.

A minimum viable office can stand up in about 3 to 6 months, and the first 90 days should already report early wins. The way to start is with a maturity assessment of the client's existing governance, a ruthlessly simple design, tracking business value rather than activity, and visible executive sponsorship from the outset. Where the office reports matters more than almost any other design choice: the strongest models anchor to the CFO or finance, with impact verified as booked monthly, because finance sign off separates value that is real from value merely claimed by whoever built the case. The natural shape is to build it with the client, run it together for a period, and hand it over, on a build, operate and transfer basis; the exit is the whole point, since an office the client can run alone becomes a reference, while one it can never run without help becomes a liability.

Five anti patterns are worth naming to a client directly, because naming them is itself a credibility signal. Benefit tracking theatre is dashboards full of vanity metrics tracking nothing anyone signed up to own, the pattern the merger scorecard finding above illustrates best. Post project amnesia is the team disbanding at go live with nobody left holding the benefit. The wallpaper business case is something signed at approval that is already irrelevant by month two; the private equity version becomes a document nobody updates after the second quarter of year one. Ownership by implication is a benefit with no named owner, no baseline and no consequence attached. And a value office that acts like the police instead of a coach gets gamed or ignored, when the entire point is to help owners hit their number.

### 8. The twelve thematics of the field

Realisation is where value dies, and it is measured, since the loss concentrates at target setting and after go live rather than during delivery, because delivery discipline improved for decades while benefits discipline never did.

Ownership is the field's most repeated finding, since across the benefits discipline, the transformation studies and deal practice, a benefit with a named owner in the business arrives and an unowned one does not, and ownership beats method every time it gets tested.

Measurement decays unless it lives inside rhythms that already exist, since separate benefits tracking dies within months and only tracking that sits inside dashboards and meetings leaders already attend survives.

Two schools answer the same problem: one says value fails for lack of baselines and tracking, and the other says it fails because behaviour never changed, with the strongest evidence, such as the finding that 70 per cent of AI value comes from people and process change, showing that the strongest practices run both while most firms sell only one.

The definition of value was never settled, since financial value is the only dimension every authority names, the public sector and the benefits discipline treat customer, people, risk and societal value as equals, and the field splits on whether to monetise everything into one number or judge a scorecard in native units, and on whether to measure the technology or the business.

Optimism bias is institutionalised to the point that governments formally inflate every business case to correct for it, which quietly concedes that cases as written cannot be trusted on their own.

The value office is the market's converged product, since nearly every large firm sells the same shape of a diagnostic, then a value tree, then an embedded office anchored to finance, with value leakage as the shared enemy and a tracking layer now expected, while fees drift toward outcomes and stall on proof.

Vendors run value machinery that stops at signature, since value engineering exists at every major platform to win the sale, and after close nobody owns realisation, a gap visible across every vendor studied.

Governance turned in 2026, since boards now gate further funding on proof of return, value reporting is heading toward a board level standard, and visibility plus accountability measurably multiply realised value, meaning the question moved from the programme office to the boardroom.

The lifecycle is the discipline's native shape, whether it is identify, execute and sustain, or plan, in flight and post implementation, or opportunity through steady state, because every serious body of work organises realisation across the life of an investment with a standing function sitting above it.

Underuse is the quiet evidence base, since half of licensed capability sits unused, teams run work in spreadsheets beside platforms that could do it, cloud waste is rising again, and the cost discipline that grew up around this is now widening into a value discipline.

Deals are the most quantified arena in the whole field, since synergy overestimation is systemic, cost synergies land while revenue synergies mostly do not, speed of integration correlates with capture, and the hundred day operating pattern is the most mature realisation practice the field has produced.

And vendor commissioned studies of their own return on investment, such as a platform claiming 417 per cent or another claiming 251 per cent, are only usable as a description of the value the vendor itself claims is available, never as independent evidence of what actually happened.
