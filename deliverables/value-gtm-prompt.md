Here is what my research to date has established, so you do not start from nothing. Realising value is the industry's weak point and it is measured: only 0.5 per cent of sixteen thousand studied projects hit cost, time and benefits together (Flyvbjerg 2023); about a quarter of transformation value is lost at the business case and target setting stage (McKinsey); only 39 per cent of organisations mostly or always deliver their full project benefits (Wellingtone 2025); transformations with someone dedicated to owning the value capture 24 per cent more of it (Bain 2024, twenty four thousand initiatives). The pressure is now at board level: two thirds of boards make further AI funding conditional on proof of return, board level value reporting is expected to become standard for large enterprises by the end of 2026 (Deloitte), and organisations with full visibility of their AI costs are five times more likely to show an established return (KPMG 2026). Nobody agrees what value is: financial value is the only dimension every firm and discipline names, the usable definitions all share the same working parts, a measure in the organisation's own terms, a baseline, a named owner and a date, and the public sector formally treats non financial value as equal to money, so value must never collapse to money alone. The market has converged on one product shape, a diagnostic, then a value tree, then an embedded value office anchored to finance, sold by PwC as a managed service and by others under different names; the platform vendors all run value engineering teams that build the case before the sale and leave once the deal closes; nobody publishes a fixed price, fixed duration entry offer; and fees across the market are drifting toward outcome based models and stalling because nobody can substantiate the outcome. On platforms specifically, roughly 46 per cent of licensed seats sit unused (Zylo 2025), more than 90 per cent of finance teams still run planning in spreadsheets beside platforms they already own, and the honest funding picture is that the client usually pays for an assessment because partner incentives mostly offset the partner's costs rather than the client's. On deals, about 70 per cent of acquirers overestimate synergies and only about 30 per cent hit their targets (Bain), and synergy timelines are usually paced by systems and data work. And the research splits into two schools, one saying value fails for lack of measurement and one saying it fails because behaviour never changes, with the strongest evidence, such as 70 per cent of AI value coming from people and process change (BCG), saying you need both. Do not use these numbers anywhere: the claim that 95 per cent of AI pilots fail, the claim that 70 per cent of transformations fail, Gartner's prediction of 30 per cent GenAI abandonment stated as fact, and vendor commissioned ROI studies presented as independent evidence.
