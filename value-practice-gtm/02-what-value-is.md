# What value is

Value is a measured improvement in an outcome the business already cares about. In full: a benefit counts as value when it is expressed in the organisation's own terms, baselined before the change begins, owned by a named person inside the operating business rather than the project team, due by a date and checked on a fixed schedule after go live, tied to a strategic priority, and framed as an outcome rather than an output. That definition is not ours by invention; it is the intersection of every usable definition the research surfaced, from the benefits discipline through the strategy firms to the public sector, and it is deliberately a test as much as a description, because most of what clients currently call benefits fails it on the first two clauses.

The definition matters commercially because nobody agrees on one. Financial value is the only dimension every firm and discipline names; beyond that, the field splits, and KPMG's own November 2025 report concedes that value creation is a concept often discussed yet rarely defined with precision, a line worth quoting back to any client who believes their organisation already has this handled. A practice that arrives with a working definition, and a quality test for benefits, is teaching from the first meeting, which is precisely the selling posture the evidence in `04-how-we-sell-it.md` supports.

## Why value, and why now

The industry's weak point is realising value, and the weakness is measured. Only 0.5 per cent of the roughly sixteen thousand projects in Flyvbjerg's database hit cost, time and benefits together; 88 per cent of transformations fail to reach their original ambition on Bain's database of more than twenty four thousand initiatives, and even the successful ones capture only 67 per cent of the value available, with about a quarter of the loss occurring at the business case stage; only 39 per cent of organisations mostly or always deliver their full project benefits on Wellingtone's 2025 survey, improving only to 42 per cent in 2026. The pressure has moved to the board: 66 per cent of boards now make further AI funding conditional on proof of return, board level AI value reporting sits at 4 per cent of organisations and is expected by Deloitte to become standard for large enterprises by the end of 2026, and organisations with full visibility of their AI costs are five times more likely to show an established return. Every figure above is verified to a primary source in `10-evidence-base.md`. The consequence for the practice is that value is no longer an internal programme management concern; it is a governance requirement clients are being examined on, which is why the conversation can now be opened without being asked.

## The dimensions, and the rule that guards them

Value has seven recurring dimensions across the field: financial, customer or experience, people, operational, risk, strategic or positional, and societal. Only the benefits discipline and the public sector treat the non financial dimensions as equal to money, and the practice should side with them, with one discipline attached: value must never collapse to money alone, and every benefit must still be owned and evidenced whatever its dimension. Cranfield's ladder makes this workable, classifying each benefit as observable, measurable, quantifiable or financial, so that a benefit can be legitimate without being monetised provided it is placed honestly on the ladder and given an owner. Keeping some benefits deliberately non financial also turns out to be a defence against gaming, which is argued in `07-proving-value.md`.

## The quality test for a benefit

The research produced one genuinely underused asset here: Zwikael and Chih's validated criteria for a well formed target benefit, which nobody in the consulting market has productised. A benefit passes when it has strategic fit, a target value, measurability, realism, an accountable owner, a target date, and comprehensiveness, meaning the set of benefits covers the investment's whole intent. The practice should turn these seven criteria into a scored gate applied to every benefit in every value case, because it converts what value is from a philosophy into a checklist a client can watch their own business case fail, which is the single most persuasive moment a diagnostic can produce.

## The four forks, each with a position

The field genuinely disagrees on four questions, and the practice needs a stated position on each, because a client will eventually ask.

**Whose value is it, shareholders' or every stakeholder's?** Follow the client's own strategy documents; if their strategy commits to customers, people or communities, their value case may carry those dimensions with owners and measures, and if their strategy is shareholder return, the case anchors there. The practice adjudicates quality of measurement, as opposed to ideology.

**Monetise everything into one number, or keep a scorecard in native units?** Recommend the scorecard with a financial spine: the headline financial case in money, the remaining benefits in their own units on the Cranfield ladder. Monetising everything manufactures false precision that finance teams discount on sight; a scorecard without any financial spine never survives the CFO. Decide the exact balance per client, following the language their board already uses.

**Measure the value of the technology, or the value to the business?** Always the business. Cranfield's Benefits Dependency Network is the structural fix, separating the means, which is the technology, from the ways, which is the business change, from the ends, which are the benefits; its doctrine, that technology delivers no benefit and only business change does, is the sentence that separates this practice from every vendor value team it will be compared with.

**Is value a ratio or a delta?** Some boards think in benefit over resources, others in surplus above the cost of capital. Speak whichever dialect the board already speaks; the working parts of the definition survive either arithmetic, and converting a board to a new arithmetic is a fight that wins nothing.

One further framing tool earns its place in the first meeting: the Four Ares from Val IT, asking whether we are doing the right things, doing them the right way, getting them done well, and getting the benefits. The framework it came from is dead; the four questions survive because audit committees recognise them instantly, and the fourth is the one nobody in the room can answer, which is the practice's opening.
