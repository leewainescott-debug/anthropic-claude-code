# Where we disagree

This pack was commissioned with a set of working beliefs, and the instruction was to build on them and challenge them rather than treat them as settled. Six places deserve a direct challenge, each at the level of whether we have the right problem, the right solution or the right articulation, and each closes with a recommended position rather than a menu.

## One: the living and breathing business case should die on purpose

The commissioning belief is that business cases today are just set up to get the dollars and then go nowhere, and should be turned into living and breathing baseline and metric tracking sources used throughout the engagement. The diagnosis is right and the evidence for it is strong; about a quarter of transformation value is lost at the business case and target setting stage, and governments formally inflate business cases for optimism bias, which quietly concedes that cases as written cannot be trusted. The prescription needs one refinement, because the research pushes back on forever in three ways. Jenner's benefits discipline holds that benefits should migrate into the business's normal performance management, meaning the operational scorecard is the living document and the case is scaffolding. Goodhart's law and the metrics gaming literature warn that a permanently tracked number tied to funding becomes a managed number, which is how deals fail while their trackers stay green. And the escalation research shows a periodically re presented case can be framed to sustain commitment to a failing investment as easily as to correct it. The recommended position: the value case lives through delivery, rebaselined at every funding gate with money released in tranches against evidence, and then dies on purpose after go live, with each benefit transferring into its owner's everyday scorecard and the case retired. The instinct survives fully through delivery, where it matters most; the refinement is that the case's ending is designed, and the ending is itself the proof that value became operational. This position is also more sellable, because a CFO will fund scaffolding with a demolition date sooner than a permanent new reporting artefact.

## Two: the candidate offerings mixed two kinds of thing, which is why the structure kept failing

The commissioned candidate list, a value realisation office, value tracking with reporting cadence and governance, AI value, unlocking platforms already owned, and post merger value, mixes offers with arenas. An office and a tracking cadence are things a client buys; AI, owned platforms and mergers are contexts a client buys them in, and any structure that puts both kinds on one list will overlap, because every arena needs every offer. The recommended position, built fresh from the problem as instructed, is the grid in `03-what-we-sell.md`: four offers on the lifecycle of an investment, four arenas they sell into. Every candidate on the original list survives inside it; the office is offer four, tracking and governance live inside offers two and four, and AI, platforms and mergers are three of the four arenas, with transformations completing the set.

## Three: the office is the destination, and the diagnostic is the door

The commissioning energy leans toward the value realisation office as the flagship. The market evidence says the office is what the whole market has converged on selling, and the practice building evidence says a first engagement should be small, fixed and low risk, converting roughly one in three; an office is the largest possible first ask, made with no evidence base, into a market where PwC, Capgemini and McKinsey already sell office shaped products with brands the practice cannot outspend. The recommended position is to sell the fixed price value case first, let assurance and recovery build the evidence, and sell the office to clients who have already seen the discipline work, on build, operate and transfer terms with decision rights or not at all.

## Four: the technology relationship is the asset, as opposed to the limitation

The commissioning frame treats today's technology side relationships as the constraint, with value conversations belonging to the CEO, the CFO and the COO. The research inverts this: the CIO currently holds the most acute version of the proof problem, with 74 per cent believing their role is at risk without measurable gains, and the CFO enters most credibly as co author of verification rather than as the target of a cold pitch. The recommended position is in `05-beyond-the-cto.md`: go through the technology relationship, let benefit ownership and finance verification force the wider executive relationships into existence, and decline the board altitude fight where MBB and the Big Four structurally win.

## Five: clients will buy relief from proof, more than they will buy value as an idea

The commissioning question how do I sell value assumes value is the thing being sold. The evidence says the thing clients recognise as a purchasable problem in 2026 is proof: boards gating funding, finance leaders asked for numbers they cannot produce, CFOs and CIOs colliding over accountability with no shared machinery. The recommended articulation across every asset the practice publishes is therefore proof language before value language; the practice sells the ability to know, show and stand behind what an investment did to the business, and value realisation is the discipline that delivers it. This is an articulation change more than a substance change, and articulation is where sales are won.

## Six: measurement alone repeats the market's favourite failure

The commissioning frame is strongly measurement shaped, baselines, metrics, tracking, cadence. The research's most consistent warning, from the academic critique that rational process alone by and large fails to deliver, through the change discipline's evidence, to the finding that 70 per cent of AI value comes from redesigned people and process, is that the field splits into a measurement school and a behaviour school and the strongest practitioners run both. A practice that sells only the measurement school will produce beautifully instrumented value that never arrives. The recommended position is structural rather than rhetorical: behaviour fluency is one of the three capabilities in `03-what-we-sell.md`, adoption and process change appear inside every value case as the enabling changes benefits depend on, and the proof system measures behaviour change as leading indicators inside the benefit chain, so the two schools are one method rather than a debate.

## Where the research strengthened the commissioning instincts

Fairness requires the other column. The instinct that value must never collapse to money alone is fully supported, and sharpened by the Cranfield ladder and the Goodhart defence. The instinct to reframe business cases into value cases is supported by the strongest numbers in the field, and the public sector turns out to hold the best raw material for productising it, from optimism bias corrections through tranche funding to benefit quality gates, all of it published, citable and unclaimed by any commercial firm. The instinct that proving is where everyone falls over is confirmed everywhere the research looked, and it is the reason the recommended identity leads with evidence wired into platforms. And the instinct to open with what value does this investment drive for our business as a whole is exactly the reframe the taught insight motion needs; it survives as the first question of the first meeting.
