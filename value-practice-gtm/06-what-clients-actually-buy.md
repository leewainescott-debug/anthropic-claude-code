# What clients actually buy

Clients pay for measurement attached to money already in motion, and they pay for it bundled with delivery or wrapped around delivery as assurance; the evidence for value work sold as a standalone product is real but small. That single sentence should discipline the whole commercial design, because the temptation in a value practice is to sell the discipline in the abstract, and the abstract does not sign.

## The evidence on what signs

The only public record of signed prices for standalone benefits work is UK government procurement, and it says the tickets are modest: a benefits management maturity review at fifty nine thousand nine hundred and ninety pounds, transformation benefits assurance at seventy four thousand pounds, a benefits management improvement plan at two hundred and forty one thousand pounds, strategic benefits management at eighty two thousand pounds for around four and a half months, and a three month benefits realisation initiation at forty one thousand pounds. Published rate cards for benefits realisation people run from three hundred and fifty to eleven hundred pounds per day, which prices an embedded value office head at roughly one hundred and eighty to two hundred and fifty thousand pounds a year at the senior end. In the private sector the same work overwhelmingly signs attached to something larger; PwC's value realisation managed services sell as embedded services with milestone and shared risk elements around delivery, and the research found no evidence of pure benefits tracking retainers signing at scale anywhere. Paid diagnostics convert to larger work roughly one time in three in the practitioner evidence, and high performing firms take about 70 per cent of revenue from existing clients, which together say the entry product exists to be converted, inside accounts the firm already holds.

On fee structures, what clients say and what they sign differ in a useful way: about 79 per cent of executives say their consulting contracts include some outcome element and 88 per cent want more, while what actually signs is hybrid, a fixed base with a bounded at risk share, because pure gain share dies on attribution and baseline disputes. The practice should read that as demand for shared risk it is uniquely positioned to satisfy later, once its own baselines make outcomes contractible, and as a warning never to lead with contingency.

## What clients will pay for, in order of evidence strength

First, assurance and recovery attached to live spend they are already anxious about: a programme at a gate, an AI portfolio the board is questioning, a platform renewal where the CFO can see the unused half of the licence bill. The anxiety is the budget line. Second, the paid entry diagnostic, provided it is small, fixed and produces a standalone deliverable worth having even if nothing follows. Third, embedded senior capacity in an office, at senior programme rates, once a diagnostic or an assurance engagement has established trust. Fourth, and only later, fees at risk as a share of value the practice itself baselined.

## What clients will not pay for

They will not pay for benefits tracking as a standalone subscription detached from any investment, because the private sector record of such deals is effectively empty. They will not pay for a value office as a first engagement, because it is the largest ask with the least evidence. They will not pay for outcome contingent work without an agreed attribution protocol, and attempting it produces disputes rather than fees. And they will not pay for what vendors give away: Salesforce's Business Value Services builds investment cases free before the deal, most platform health checks are free lead generation, and Microsoft's FastTrack is genuinely free, so any offer that resembles a pre sale case or a checklist health check will be compared to free and lose. The practice sells what happens after signature, which nobody gives away because nobody else owns it.

## Who pays, from which budget

Three funding lines exist, and the strongest engagements chain them. The client's own change or programme budget is the default for the value case and assurance, and the CFO's emerging governance agenda is the natural home for office funding, since value offices are increasingly created inside finance functions. Vendor partner funds are the second line, and 2025 and 2026 have made them structural: AWS migration funding runs to hundreds of thousands of dollars at scale with assessment phases effectively vendor funded, Snowflake's services funds run at 3 to 5 per cent with referral economics on top, Salesforce's 2026 program deploys consumption funds that reward long term adoption, which is the exact funding line for helping a client use what they already bought, and Databricks ties partner incentives to consumption. Funded vehicles usually cannot be stacked but can be chained, an assessment first and a funded phase behind it. The honest picture to give a client is that partner incentives mostly offset the partner's cost of delivery rather than functioning as a grant to the client, and honesty here is cheap credibility. The third line is the practice's double earning: the same evidenced consumption and adoption outcomes that the client pays for also move Slalom's partner scorecards and unlock incentive economics, which means the practice can price entry work aggressively without pricing it desperately.

## The consequence for the offer set

The commercial design in `03-what-we-sell.md` follows this evidence directly: a small paid entry that converts, assurance and recovery attached to live spend, an office sold only on evidence, hybrid fees with at risk shares earned rather than promised, and vendor funding chained under the platforms arena. Nothing in the offer set asks a client to buy something the record says clients do not buy.
