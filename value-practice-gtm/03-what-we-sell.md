# What we sell

We sell one discipline, realising value across the life of an investment, productised into four offers that follow the lifecycle and sold into four arenas where demand already exists. The offers are the value case, value assurance, value recovery and the value office; the arenas are AI investments, platforms already owned, deals, and transformations. This grid replaces the earlier candidate list, which mixed offers with arenas and therefore could never be made mutually exclusive; a value realisation office is a thing a client buys, while AI value is a context a client buys it in, and holding those apart is what makes the architecture hold together. The lifecycle is mutually exclusive in time and collectively exhaustive over an investment's life, so nothing a client needs falls between two offers, and every arena is served by all four offers, so nothing about the structure has to change when the next arena arrives.

## The four offers

### One: the value case, before the money is committed

A fixed price, fixed duration diagnostic, three weeks, that takes a live business case, or an investment about to seek one, and rebuilds it as a value case. The deliverables are a value tree linking the investment to the strategic priorities it drives, expressed in the board's own measures; a baseline for every benefit, taken from real operating data and, wherever the investment touches AWS, Salesforce, Snowflake or Databricks, from the platforms' own usage and cost data; a named owner and a date for every benefit, gated by the seven part quality test in `02-what-value-is.md`; the kill conditions, meaning what evidence at what gate would stop the investment; a funding design that releases money in tranches against evidenced outcomes, which is the mechanic the New Zealand Treasury has published most cleanly and no private sector firm has productised; and the measurement plan that makes everything above checkable, including what will be instrumented on which platform. It is priced as a published fixed fee, recommended between sixty and ninety thousand dollars, because no competitor in any tier publishes a fixed price entry offer and the absence is a differentiator on its own; the pricing evidence is in `06-what-clients-actually-buy.md`.

The value case is the door to everything else, and it is also the reframe made physical: the client asked for a document that gets the dollars, and receives a document that governs whether the dollars were worth it. Its intended life is stated inside it: it is rebaselined at every funding gate through delivery, and it dies on purpose after go live, with each benefit transferring into its owner's everyday scorecard; the reasoning for the planned death is in `09-where-we-disagree.md`.

### Two: value assurance, while the money is being spent

A governance engagement over a live programme, priced by milestone, that makes the gates kill capable. It rebaselines the value case at every stage gate so that each gate revalidates the benefits and never only the budget; it runs the operating cadence the deal world has proven, a weekly delivery forum, a monthly value review ending in a decision to go, hold, kill or accelerate, and a quarterly review with executives; it holds the tranche funding design, releasing money against evidence the way venture funding works; and it applies the decision architecture the research says gates need to actually kill anything, meaning independent gatekeepers and kill criteria committed in advance, because the Sony Ericsson evidence shows gates without that architecture get talked past. Assurance is also where the practice most naturally attaches to programmes other firms are delivering, as the independent party verifying value over someone else's build, which the market evidence says is a shape clients pay for.

### Three: value recovery, after go live

The realisation phase itself, and the least contested ground in the market, since the vendors' value teams have left and the strategy firms rarely descend to it. Two engagement patterns share the offer. The first is the post implementation review the field skips most: a check of benefit realised against target a few weeks to a few months after go live, plus the hunt for further benefits that Cranfield's method mandates as its final phase and consultancies universally drop. The second is the platforms already owned pattern, a two to three week diagnostic reconciling three views of a platform, what was contracted, what was deployed, and what is actually used, since the gaps between those views cut both ways as audit exposure and reclaimable value; the evidence base is broad, with 46 per cent of SaaS licences unused at an average waste of 19.8 million dollars per enterprise per year, cloud waste rising to 29 per cent of spend in 2026, 80 per cent of software features rarely or never used, and more than 90 per cent of finance practitioners still planning in spreadsheets beside platforms they already own. Week one reconciles the three views and runs the vendor's own tooling as a baseline; week two maps findings to where the business is going over the next twelve to twenty four months, so priorities attach to real outcomes rather than licence dollars; week three delivers a roadmap tying every item to a named business outcome, sorted into turn on now or needs a scoped enablement phase, which is the natural conversion point into further work, and closes with a measurement cadence so the client keeps an operating discipline rather than a report. Independence is the credibility angle; a firm that does not sell the licences has less reason to shade the finding.

### Four: the value office, standing above the lifecycle

The market's converged product, sold our way. The office is built with the client, run together, and handed over on a build, operate and transfer basis, because an office the client can run alone becomes a reference while one they can never run without us becomes a liability. It anchors to the CFO, with impact verified with finance on a monthly cadence, because finance sign off is what separates value that is real from value claimed by whoever built the case. It carries decision rights, over funding tranches, kill recommendations and resourcing, or it is not sold at all, because the strongest critique in the research is that a value office without authority is theatre that produces slides about progress; and it behaves as a coach to benefit owners rather than as police, because a policing office gets gamed or ignored. A minimum viable office stands up in three to six months and reports early wins inside ninety days. Its design includes its own dissolution: benefits migrate into the business's normal performance management, which is Jenner's principle and the planned death argument again, so the office shrinks as the client's own value culture grows, and the exit becomes the proof.

## The four arenas

The arenas are where demand already exists, and each one chooses the door, the urgency and the funding line, while the offers stay the same underneath.

**AI investments** carry the loudest 2026 urgency: boards gating funding on proof, only 7 per cent of finance organisations reporting high impact from AI, the top fifth of companies capturing 74 per cent of all AI economic value, and BCG's finding that 70 per cent of AI value comes from redesigning people and process. The practice sells the value case before the AI spend, assurance over the portfolio, and recovery over the pilots that never proved anything.

**Platforms already owned** is the quiet arena where the evidence is strongest and the entry cheapest, often vendor funded; it suits clients wanting value without new capital, exactly the unlock without paying millions to upgrade instinct this practice was founded on.

**Deals** are the most quantified arena in the field, with about 70 per cent of acquirers overestimating synergies, only 43 per cent running any synergy tracking process, and 50 to 60 per cent of synergy initiatives dependent on IT and data work, which is Slalom's home ground; the hundred day operating pattern is the most mature realisation practice anywhere, and Slalom already has a Synergy Tracker built with DealRoom to anchor on. West Monroe owns the private equity version of this arena, so the recommended entry is corporate acquirers first.

**Transformations** are the broadest arena and the one where the value office most naturally lands, since a transformation with someone dedicated to owning the value captures 24 per cent more of it on Bain's evidence.

## The differentiator: the evidence runs on the platforms' own data

Every serious competitor sells a diagnostic, a value tree and an office; the shape is converged and cannot differentiate. What differentiates is where the evidence comes from. MBB value offices run on manually reported programme status; this practice wires baselines and tracking into the usage, adoption and cost data of the platforms Slalom builds on, which makes the evidence cheaper to collect, harder to dispute, and impossible for a firm outside the build to replicate. The research found no competitor doing this well across more than one vendor. It also means the same evidence earns twice, because all four ecosystems restructured their partner economics in 2025 and 2026 to pay for exactly this: Salesforce's 2026 program overhaul deploys roughly a billion dollars of incentives including consumption funds rewarding long term adoption, AWS's migration funding scales with projected consumption and its new agentic AI specialisations reward proven outcomes, Snowflake's partner scorecard is consumption growth and its services funds run at 3 to 5 per cent, and Databricks' new partner network ties incentives to consumption. A practice that proves client value in platform data simultaneously moves every partner scorecard Slalom is measured on, so the practice strengthens the alliance economics of the whole firm, which is a load bearing argument in the internal case in `08-the-first-year.md`.

## The capability

The team is senior heavy by design, because a new expertise practice runs on experience rather than on large teams of junior people in its first years, and it carries three fluencies that rarely sit in one person: finance fluency, to build cases a CFO signs and to run verification with finance; platform fluency, to wire measurement into the four ecosystems and speak to what was contracted, deployed and used; and behaviour fluency, to design the adoption and process change that the evidence says carries most of the value, since the strongest research finding in the field is that measurement without behaviour change fails exactly as often as behaviour change without measurement. Selling only the measurement school would repeat the market's own favourite failure.

The assets to build in year one are the benefit quality gate from Zwikael's criteria; a value tree library per arena; telemetry adapters per platform, meaning the repeatable wiring from each platform's usage and cost data into a value scoreboard; the attribution protocol template that gets agreed at contract signing; the case death checklist that moves benefits into owners' scorecards; and the charter client case studies with contractual reference rights. Slalom already publishes a Business Value Framework, an AI business value calculator, the DealRoom Synergy Tracker, and outcome quantified case studies across the platforms; the practice's first internal act should be consolidating those scattered assets under one owner, because the concept already lives in Slalom's language and the missing thing is a practice that owns the method.
